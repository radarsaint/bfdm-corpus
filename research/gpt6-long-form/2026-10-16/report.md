C. Creative design and mechanical method
Early Roanoke: a deliberately difficult colony becomes a bookkeeping burden
The 2018 Rowing Oak source BCS-000136 includes many town professions, a player-elected mayor and charter, offline production every two hours, food requirements, and an explicit refusal to fill every town function automatically. Scarcity was meant to make other people valuable. The 2019 Season 2 outline BCS-000042, with contemporaneous revision support discussed in existing research/longitudinal/roanoke-economy-2018-to-s2.md, contains Brendon's explicit self-critique: players counting hours not playing “felt like a chore”; banning currency to encourage cooperation “removed the language of trade” while players cooperated anyway. High-interest professions attracted players; essential carpenter roles remained empty. His proposal is daily crafting attempts, vocation bonuses, banking time, quality dice, returning currency and using more NPCs for absent professions. The proposed replacement is not proof of a successful delivered economy.
This exposes a distinguishable creative competence: he recognizes that a simulation mechanism may reproduce a plausible world yet fail as an activity, and he can reconsider a mechanism without abandoning the desired underlying social relationship. It also documents two real original-design mistakes. This is already an established research case; the new contribution here is the comparison with other formats, not ownership of the 2018→2019 finding.

F. Comparison and provisional synthesis
There is strong documentary evidence of creative range: physical-activity conversion, persistent settlement simulation, urban multi-DM systems, investigative procedural campaigns, authored character interaction, and contemporary spectacle-comedy. It is not evidence that every format worked in use.
The more useful comparison concerns what Brendon is willing to change:
In 2019 Roanoke, he considers changing a game's economic machinery when it makes players do unpleasant accounting or fails to promote the intended cooperation.
In 2020 S3, he updates his encounter expectation after player-supplied resources, and separately corrects a balance mistake without undoing a night's completed outcomes.
In 2021 Empire City, he permits meaningful rebuilding outside original prep while retaining narrative ownership and waiting for the player to act.
In 2026 Earthfall, he treats an attempted combat circumvention as desirable and seeks a corresponding urgent puzzle.
In 2026 Saturday D&D, he insists that finding a way out of a stalled clue sequence may not give an NPC facts the players never told him.
These do NOT establish one universal "always improvise" preference. The conditioning variables differ: player autonomy, world consistency, the function of a rule, narrative legibility, resource truth, schedule, knowledge asymmetry, and the history already accepted.
There is also a tension: the 2018 Roanoke guide scripts emotionally important beats and a macro historical catastrophe while inviting local choices and asking for feedback. The 2026 Earthfall instructions reject prescripted encounters, and Saturday instructions reject convenient NPC omniscience. This might indicate creative development, differences between campaign formats, or simply differences between preparing a campaign and reacting to a live scene. Chronology alone does not decide which. Nor can a single S3 live NPC establish Brendon's overall improvisational performance.
What remains genuinely unknown: ordinary action distribution and how often DM intentionally does nothing; actual performance in voice sessions; whether proposed fixes worked; how to attribute coauthored setting language; how Season 5 and Earthfall decisions shaped player long-term satisfaction; how modern Kit corrections predict real autonomous DM competence.
