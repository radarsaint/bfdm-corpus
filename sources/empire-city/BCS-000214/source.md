Having never been a mason, The Mender doesn’t understand how he is being tapped to rebuild them.  The Scales and Feather is the deity that the brothers followed, thus is a major deity for the masons in general, and therefore provides an avenue for the Mender to step up, and inspire hope, life, and love into the people in the world.  But why can’t the Final Judge find the souls of Daysong and Hiram.  Did they not pass on?  If they didn't, why aren’t they tending to the capstones in Arcania?  How much time has passed?

The Mender laments his friends, while still resenting them.  If they hadn't abandoned him, maybe he wouldn’t have spent centuries in an endless war.  The Mender shakes his head, resolving to help, regardless of how he ended.  Petitioning for info on the Masons, he asks the Scales and Feather to grant him the wisdom to accomplish his task, and the Scales and Feather summons forth a faceless one, the one called "Raconteur".  In an endless meadow they walk and trade stories.  They speak of love and lives lost, and how the fear of those losses ruled the minds and hearts of the lost island.  The Mender finds that the death of the Order of Masons is more incomplete then their enemies would believe.  Hiram had accepted the task of engraving a golden cornerstone. His defeat at the hands of his brother’s had driven him to work in seclusion as a hermit until his new work was completed.  Although the Raconteur hasn’t been to Arcania in ages, the Mason, John Harvard, proved to be a poor leader, broken by the death he had witnessed. The Masons of the New World faded, forgetting their sacred duty.

Their duty as best as the Raconteur had gathered, was to stabilize magic, using some sort of rune magic to make objects to act as some sort of anchor.  This in turn would help keep otherworld evils (and possibly goods?) from tainting the land, and enslaving ‘mankind’

The Raconteur cannot stay in one place too long.  He has been spending all of time trying to slip past death in every plane of existence.  However, he promises that in a time of need, he will show, and tell the new masons, the story of Roanoke as best he knows.

With that the Raconteur takes his leave.

So the Mender, as ready as he can be, tells the Scales and Feather he is ready.  The Scales and Feather gives two warnings.  First, as the Mender is a child of war, he may only cross between death and life when the voices of the dead sing the lament of war. Just as he departed in war, so shall he return.  Second, the fae court will build the Mender a new body, but will extract a heavy toll of favors in return.  Be careful what you grant, in the fae court words are currency,

The Mender shakes his head, yet another war, more unnessesairy death, more people treating life as disposable for some stupid cause.  “No time like the present to get started.”
