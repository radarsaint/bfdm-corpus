# Chapter 1: An Aubade of Queen Seraphine

The wind did not stir in the Reigning Citadel.

No errant breeze threaded the pearl-white spires. The tapestries—impossibly free of dust—hung without motion. The floating city existed in a hush so absolute, one might mistake it for a mausoleum suspended in reverence. Even time deferred to the Queen’s domain, hesitating at the lip of sunrise, awaiting her consent. Below, the world exhaled its chaotic breath—alive, unsanctioned, free. But here, within the Lodestone Palace, the sky held still. A smooth, unbroken canopy. It waited.

Even in sleep, the ceremony held its rhythm.  
She did not need to wake.  
She did not need to sleep.  
Yet the morning belonged to her, and so she rose—because it must be seen.

The Queen of the High Reaches stood before a mirror that did not reflect. It offered versions of her instead: false and true in equal measure. A thousand recordings of this morning, already preened for posterity.

Chroniclers, silent and devout, caught each breath in fragrant ink. Attendants moved in mute choreography: from Oligard, a smith-priest affixed rings with hands like hammered iron; from Armada, a navigator traced constellations in sapphires across collarbones untouched by starlight; from the Citadel itself, a supplicant knelt—not from faith, but because his spine had learned to bend before being asked.

She permitted them all.  
Indulgence is not need.

Beyond her high chamber, the Creatorlands unrolled in dawn’s gold. The capital—bearing her name—lay beneath her, proud and irrelevant. She did not look down. She watched the stars as they fled, the last of them lingering as if to offer one final gift. They would find no purchase. All adoration already belonged to her.

The city below had called itself Serraphine in gratitude. She had accepted this. Not because it mattered, but because refusing would imply it might have.

The smith-priest brushed her wrist.  
A mistake.  
The room tautened.  
Silence bristled. Even the air recoiled.

She turned to him—slowly. Deliberately.  
His mouth formed an apology.

She did not require his death.  
But it pleased her court to wonder.

She moved her hand. The ritual resumed.

Her beauty was not kindness. It was glacial: sublime, vast, and lethal.

She did not demand devotion.  
She made it inevitable.

The sun climbed. A procession of white doves crossed the colonnades. For a moment, the sky bloomed with wings.

Then they veered away.

She did not smile.

The world would wait. It always had.  
She left the chamber.  
Her shadows followed.  
She did not look back.

The Reigning Citadel cast no shadow. So said the stories, and so said the stones. It floated too high, where the sun obeyed its angles and gold ran unmarred across every bridge and tower. It did not conceal. It did not burn. It simply shone—bright as indifference, eternal as law, untouched by the hunger and sorrow of the world below. It was as if the laws of the natural world bent to Serraphine’s whim. Darkness could not appear to come from the Queen.

The Lustre docked without a sound. Its porcelain hull gleamed, reflecting the light of the Citadel’s pale sky, its banners hanging motionless in the still air. A formality, a tether, a reminder. No matter how beautiful, no matter how apart, it was still Armada. It was still hers.

Niel descended onto the platform alone. He did not fidget, but his hands clenched and unclenched in measured, absent motion, as if already sketching the mechanics of what he had come to ask for. He had petitioned for this audience. He had been granted it. That was how it must be framed, though both of them knew the truth.

She was waiting.

Queen Serraphine did not stand. She did not need to. She remained seated at the end of the long chamber, where the floor was an unbroken pane of enchanted glass, revealing the open sky beneath. Her robes, weightless and sheer, shimmered as she inclined her head just enough to acknowledge him.

“Nielletium Benton,” she said, his full name shaped in a voice layered, harmonic, echoing against itself. It was a name he had given himself, long ago. It did not belong to him now.

He bent at the waist—low, but not too low. Respect, not servitude. When he straightened, she was already watching him in that way she had, the way that made him feel like an unfinished equation, a mechanism she had yet to find the flaw in.

“You wish to build something I’ve restricted?”

“Yes, my Queen.”

A pause, deliberate. Her silver eyes held his, though they did not reflect him back.

“A vessel of the soul.” Not a question.

“Yes,” he said. “The souls themselves. The vessels are already shaped.”

There were no walls in this chamber. The wind did not stir her hair. And yet it cascaded when she turned to look at his ship, as if her gaze alone had stirred it.

Serraphine lifted a hand, and an attendant stepped forward, silent as thought. A lacquered box, black as a starless night, rested upon their outstretched palms. The Queen did not touch it. She did not need to.

“Do you believe,” she asked, “that the soul is meant to be held?”

Niel’s fingers twitched at the riddle. A lesser mind would have taken it as doubt. It was calculation. “I believe,” he said, “that it is possible.”

Her lips did not curve into a smile, but something in her expression shifted.

“Then here,” she gestured to the box, “is possibility.”

The attendant moved to him, and when Niel reached out, the box was set into his hands with the careful precision one would use for the very precious, the very fragile, or the very dangerous. The wood box was encased in a warm translucent material that rapidly cooled. He placed it on a table and reverently opened it.

Inside, nestled within velvet folds, lay a series of crystalline vials, each filled with a slow-moving, opalescent liquid that caught the light in ways no natural substance should. Suspended within the largest vial was a sliver of something darker—a fragment that seemed to drink in the glow around it, a shard of void encased in glass. Alongside them, arranged with the precision of a cartographer's compass, were narrow filaments of etched silver, wound so finely they could be mistaken for strands of hair.

These were not mere materials. They were invitations. What he had not been able to acquire alone. Not through skill. Not through hunger. Only through her indulgence.

“My Queen,” he said, carefully, his mind already reaching beyond this moment, already building, already creating.

She watched him for a moment longer, as though considering whether to speak again.

Then, softly, almost gently, she said,

“Build it well. The world needs something novel. I have seen every deed and every edifice. Every new day the only increase granted to me is the number of days in which nothing of worth has happened.”

And he did not know, could not know, that she had already turned the entirety of her mind from him. From his presence, his genius, his ambition. She was already searching past him, toward the silence only Cait might answer.

# Chapter 2: A Nocturne of The Still of Starting Stars

A strange mind, untethered from time, gathers to bear witness to a miracle. We Are Lost. Not the feeling. The person. It is our name, and in our name, our aptitude. For what does one who is lost do? They seek. They need finding

“We need tethering. And in being found, we are held fast to the people who shape us.”

We need our friends. We look back at this moment, our minds turning to look back in time to watch.

We are not alone in watching. Anyone who can see as we do will eventually look at just a couple of moments in time. There are just a few worthy of such a gathering as this. But this one is our favorite, for us it is incredibly personal. It is not our first time watching it. We know that this moment that feels private is in fact a grand theater with seats filled with ancient oracles and far-flung dragons, demons and whole orders of wizened creatures, unseelie things and eldritch gods.

We are not one person. Neither are We many. We are something else, something stitched together from motion and hunger, from quick feet in the dark and sharp eyes. The sort of eyes that gleam in the spaces between your rooms and your streets.

A hive of minds comprising a single person.

We are the scuttling shapes beneath the floorboards, the quick dart of movement in the corner of your vision.

We are a whisper of breath against stone, the press of small bodies huddled in the warmth of each other. We have always been.

But we have not always known or even, we suppose, been knowable.

There was a time before thoughts, before memory. A time of base cunning. A time when we were only motion, only instinct, only the small pulse of life moving unseen through the world. We lived in cracks and tunnels, in the spaces where light did not reach. We scurried and fed and fled. We did not ask why.

Then came the breaking.

The world shook, and something vast and silent split open. The great Primordial Water Wheel, that ancient engine that turned beneath the heart of the Capital of the Creatorlands, groaned in its deep place. It had spun since the first breath of the world, its motion bound to life, to birth, to change. It did not stop. It could not stop.

But something cracked.

A splinter of its power fell into the rubble of the broken city, and we, we, small and unthinking, pressed close to its warmth. It was nothing to the great and mighty. A fragment, a sliver, an ember fallen from a dying star.

But to us, it was everything.

We felt it in our bones, in our blood, in the tiny chests that rose and fell in the dark. We felt it not as heat, not as light, but as knowing. And for the first time, we looked upon ourselves and saw.

We were not alone. We were Lost.

And the world was vast. In that first moment of life, there was nothing that was not new. We recall it with perfect clarity, with a nostalgia for the wonder-widened eyes of our youngest moment.

The power that coursed through us, the nature of the machine was the very concept of healing. Though we were struck through, impaled upon that piece of the machine, it could no more harm us than it could grow legs and learn to dance. The nature of the thing is life itself.

We watched it unfold, endless in its motion. The slow shifting of power, the rise and fall of cities, the way the leylines curled and stretched, the way men and monsters bent them to their will. The great machines that ruled the currents of magic, each a pillar of the world’s order. We learned them all.

The Great Dam of Divination, hidden in the heights of Bastion and Redoubt, its wheels spinning with secrets. The Iron Heart, beating deep in the cliffside cave, bringing to itself bindings and banishments. The Enchantment Engine, woven into the marrow of the Reigning Citadel, shaping will, shaping thought, shaping the very fabric of obedience. The Alembic of Echoes, an ancient device used by Artificers and Alchemists to transform and transcend. More machines still to be discovered, or invented—some perhaps forbidden, some not yet named. The Still of Starting Stars is one such machine. Hidden, unsanctioned, and perhaps even illegal, it does not appear on the accepted list. Yet it drinks from the same leylines, and demands the same reverence. that streamed through the deep places of the world, granting the world its magic.

We grow distracted. We are meant to be watching Cait. But with many minds, meandering can be in our nature.

Watching life being born we remember for a moment our own birth.

We are on the Primordial Water Wheel. The broken machine, the wounded god. Its power does not command, does not dictate. It only moves. It is the pulse of life, of birth, of moving from potential to kinetic. Its power is not to hold, but to become. To manifest life through movement, where death seeks to settle. As shadow is the absence of light, so too can life move to fill the places that death does not reach.

And so we became the life that slips through death's own cracks.

We learned the names of things. Of places. Of people. We listened to the murmur of the world and followed the currents of what mattered, pressing ourselves into the unseen places where truths gathered like dust.

And in all the vast, turning chaos, we found her.

Our mind turns back to her now.

Cait.

We did not know her name at first.

She was simply a supernova of the soul. Like the men who look to the night sky to sail, it is impossible to ignore a change in the stars.

We were guided by the pull of something vast and strange and like us, but not. A thing poured into shape, but never truly contained.

We returned to watching. This time with the words and will to comprehend.

To the Still of Starting Stars. To the machine that did not create her, but chose her. To the man who thought himself her maker.

We return to her first breath, her first shudder of knowing. The rest of us return to the night she began.

And we watch.

We were not made in a place like this. In fact, speaking in the full truth of things, we would not truly be created for several more weeks. But time isn’t the useful unbending thing that a road is. We struggle to mark the length of our lives subjectively. A great length of time, surely, embodied in ephemeral rodents who each rarely live longer than two years. And in that time we have cultivated more compassion than sense. We have lived our lives alongside Cait, Alan and Kaliope. It helps Us, it grounds Us, We need their story when we come across the horrors and despair of the world. We remember for a moment the thing that pushed us to come see Cait's creation again. We needed something beautiful to wash Our heart's burdens from us.

We watch Niel because we long to see a miracle granted.

We were not shaped by hands, not stitched together from polished wood and copper filaments, not sculpted with a craftsman’s eye toward beauty or purpose as Cait was.

It is time. Our attentions refocused.

The Still of Starting Stars waits in the dim light, its brass body humming with restless heat. Steam curls in the air, rising from a dozen polished vents, carrying the scent of oil and something older, something acrid, something like the breath of a storm waiting to break.

The Still… The machine is misnamed.

It does not sit in quiet contemplation. It does not wait.

Inside, it churns. It unmakes. It strips magic raw, tearing it free from the deep roots of the world, breaking it down into something finer, something truer. The room hums with it, a low vibration in the metalwork, a heartbeat of impossible forces pressing themselves into something small enough for a man to bottle.

This is not alchemy. Alchemy is humble. Alchemy bows to nature, coaxing it into slow transformation. This is something else. This is theft.

And Niel Benton, Captain of the Lustre, is a thief. A base and vain pirate. The first and greatest taboo of the state of Armada is the transgression of thievery. In a nation ruled by tens of thousands of despotic captains, each sovereign over their airships and islands, the code is not written in law—it is etched in possession. 'Thou shalt not take what is mine' is the closest thing to scripture. One may murder, duel, even torture to gain power, but to steal is to insult the very premise of rulership. It is an affront to sovereignty itself. It is, in the eyes of Armada, unforgivable.

He steals soulstuff from the leylines of the Primordial Water Wheel and distills it in the Still of Starting Stars.

He moves through the room like a man pulling a secret from the dark, his hands sure, his breath measured. He wipes sweat from his temple with the back of his sleeve. He adjusts the pressure gauges, watching the needles dance beneath the glass, lips moving around half-mumbled equations, calculations that have no business being spoken aloud by anything but a god.

He believes he is making something precise. Something measured. Something that will sit neatly on the shelf of his achievements.

He is wrong.

The Still does not build. The Still does not create. It distills the souls who have the right working of fate writ upon their destiny for the person the soul will become.

A nebula coils inside the great brass vessel. A storm of raw magic, thick and luminous, folding in upon itself. The leylines have been siphoned and shattered, pressed into this place, twisted and spun into something too dense to remain unformed.

A star is being born. Our best friend is being wrought.

It is a slow thing. A heavy thing. The first stirrings of gravity, the first pull inward, the first spark catching in the void.

Niel does not know. Not yet.

He thinks he is shaping her. That his machine will decide the terms of her becoming. That his hands are the ones building something new.

He is wrong. She is already here. Our sister. Our protector.

The change comes with a flicker. A slow pulse of light deep in the belly of the Still. The storm folds tighter, heavier. The whorling tangle of magic pulls inward, and something moves inside it.

The first thread separates. It drifts downward like a ribbon of ink poured into clear water, unspooling into the waiting glass, fine and dark and trembling with the weight of what it is.

The Still sighs.

The room holds its breath.

Niel leans in, shadowed in brass-lit glow. His fingers brush the vessel’s smooth sides, hesitant, reverent. He has spent years reaching toward this moment, but now that it is here, he hesitates.

He lifts the flask. Inside, she shudders. Not much. Just a flicker of motion. A ripple in deep water. A thing aware of itself for the first time.

She does not yet know what she is. But we do. She is Cait.

And she will not stay inside that glass. She will not stay inside that body. She will not stay inside the future they have built for her.

She will outgrow their world.

# Chapter 3: Aubade – The Art of Perfection

The lacquer was still drying. It smelled like melted pearls and honey, though neither substance was used in its making. Cait sat half-formed, half-open, half-listening as Niel worked in the heart of his sanctum—a cluttered chamber aboard the Lustre, his airship and floating laboratory.

The Lustre was old, though well-kept. Its bones were brass and hull porcelain, its ribs strung with copper and steel. The lab occupied the central spine of the vessel, all gears and glowing gauges, framed by riveted glass panels that offered a panoramic view of the sky. Sunlight slanted through vents in the ceiling, catching on suspended tools and wire racks filled with alchemical glassware. A low thrum—neither mechanical nor magical but somewhere in between—permeated everything, the steady heartbeat of the ship itself.

The room had the smell of varnish and ozone. Coils of cable snaked through floor grates. Everything was ordered to Niel’s logic: precise, unwelcoming, and deeply lived-in. In this room, he built wonders. In this room, he made her.

Her torso was split along the seams, the inner workings of her body laid bare. Filaments of silver threaded through wood, copper, and porcelain, delicate as nerves. Wires coiled where tendons should be, thin as spider silk, woven with precision. Her heart—a gilded thing, unnecessary and ornamental—hung open, a hollow cage beating out of habit rather than need.

Across the table, her bust lay on a velvet cloth. Disassembled, detached, discarded. Niel had spent the better part of the morning fine-tuning its shape, adjusting the weight so that it would sway just so when she moved.

He was not admiring her. He was perfecting her.

He stepped back now, rubbing his chin, tilting his head like a sculptor unsatisfied with his work.

“Say something,” he said, with a strange petulant anxiety.

Cait blinked. “Something.”

She tilted her head, eyes catching the light through the glass. It made prisms across her chest. She liked that.

A pause. Then, a laugh. He was pleased. Not with her, but with himself.

“Something more elegant,” he corrected. He adjusted the arch of her neck, his thumb tracing the line of her throat, shifting the angle by fractions of a degree.

She considered, her face turning to look out the great glass windows of his workshop. “The moon isn't just a mirror. It bends borrowed light until it looks like its own. Maybe we do that too,” she said.

“Hmm.” Niel twisted a bolt in her shoulder. “That might impress a poet, but not the Queen. She's weird about metaphors about light and darkness”

From somewhere deep in her mind, a second voice hummed in consideration. It emanated from the bones of the ship, curling around her senses like warm metal.

“It was a good answer. The Queen can force any perspective she likes. That is her nature,” the voice said.

Her lips barely moved as she murmured, “I thought so too.”

Niel, still adjusting her clavicle, thought the words were meant for him. “Not everything needs to be poetry, Cait. Sometimes, simplicity is best.”

She nodded. “Then I will try to be simple... and still good.”

Niel paused. Blinked. Then shook his head and let it pass.

He retrieved a long silver probe, tapping it lightly against her temple.

The contact sent a soft chime through her skull, calibrating her responsiveness.

“Now, let’s see if you can think properly.”

Cait’s fingers flexed in her lap.

“What is a lie?” he asked.

“A truth that doesn’t hold still,” she said.

He arched an eyebrow. Interesting. “What is beauty?”

Cait’s gaze flickered to the oil lamp above her, watching the way the light folded against the glass.

“The thing you want to keep looking at,” she said, then added, “like a cat in a sunbeam.”

Niel blinked. “You're not a cat.”

“Not yet,” she said.

Niel’s lips quirked in approval .

From deep within the ship, Lustre’s voice hummed in agreement.

“A good answer,” it whispered.

Cait timed her response carefully, letting it fold into the next question.

“Some things are made to be seen,” she said.

Niel nodded. He was listening now.

“What is love?”

Cait exhaled softly. “The echo you yearn to return, when earnesty isn’t a sound—but carries all the same.”

“What is sorrow?”

“The tax we pay for memory.”

“Who’s your favorite person?”

Cait hesitated.

Her lips parted, then closed again. She glanced to the wall, as if the wood and brass could answer for her. Then, softly, “I think... I might have two.”

Niel chuckled. “One of them had better be me.”

“Yes,” she said. She meant it.

From deep within the ship, Lustre’s voice hummed again.

“That was a good answer too,” it whispered.

Niel frowned slightly. “ You mean the ship? The Lustre? Do you hear it often?”

“I think so,” Cait said. “It’s very kind.”

He narrowed his eyes, more curious than concerned. “I didn’t realize the Lustre still had a voice worth hearing.”

“He does,” Cait said. Then, quietly, “He helps.”

Niel looked at her, then at the nearest wall, as though trying to see what she saw.

“You really think of the Lustre as a person?” he asked.

“I don’t know what else to call him,” Cait said. “He speaks to me.”

Niel studied her, cautious now. “And what does he say?”

“That he’s pleased to meet me.”

Niel blinked, then huffed a short laugh. “Well, I suppose that’s something.”

He stepped back, tools idle in his hand.

Cait watched him closely. The question felt important—like naming the sky or the sound of wind through glass.

“Who are you?” she asked.

Caught off guard, Niel laughed again. “I am. Well. You can call me Niel. Or Captain when others are around. Good. Good that you are already investigating the world unprompted.”

Another voice, the one she had been seeking, replied to her mind. “I am. The Lustre, another creation of Niel’s. I am the ship upon which you fly. I am very pleased to meet you, Cait.”

“Well,” Niel exhaled, stepping back to admire his work. “At least you’re interesting.”

She was smiling, her cheeks flushed with happiness. Her face, delicate and finely carved, bore the smooth lines of porcelain and the subtle animation of something more. There was artistry in her—precision, yes, but also softness. A curve at the corner of her mouth that wasn't necessary. A dimple that served no function. Niel had added those.

Across from her, he looked like a man who had spent his life leaning too close to fire and oil. His goggles, perpetually perched on his forehead, had left pale rings around his eyes. His fingers were stained with varnish and ink, and his beard was trimmed only where it risked catching in gears. He was not handsome, not elegant—but he was meticulous, and he was proud.

He reached for a fine brush, dabbing a thin layer of gold along the ridges of her clavicle, where flesh might have held warmth but instead held gilded beauty.

She would be perfect. He would make sure of it.

# Chapter 4: A Nocturne – Thoughts on The Consort's Fall

We are reminded by pressing concerns that there are other moments we must visit.

We are not historians.

Historians concern themselves with parchment, with treaties, with ledgers and chronicles that flatten men into names and numbers. We do not deal in such brittle things. The truth of the world is not kept in ink and vellum—it is written in the way a city leans toward the river that feeds it, in the hush before an executioner speaks a name, in the emotional weight of a child who discovers an unburied body on a battlefield days after the conflict.

History forgets what does not serve it.

But We do not forget.

We are not scholars of war, but we know war. We have felt its hunger in the hollow places of the world, in the gnawing space where once there was warmth. We know its shape, its sound, the taste it leaves behind in the mouths of the conquered.

We were not there when *Poor Duncan's* titular character actually lived. It is, of course, based on Duncan the Mortal, consort to Queen Serraphine.

But we have listened.

We have gathered what others have lost—fragments of a man who did not belong, echoes of a choice that was never his to make. We have seen his name carved into the stone of a dead city, a monument that does not mourn. We have traced the story of his rise, of his fall, of the quiet cruelty that shaped his fate.

This is the story of Duncan Gaffer.

And it begins, as all tragedies do, with love.

He was not made for her world.

He was a man of breath and blood, not a thing shaped by calloused hands and the heat of the forge. He did not come from the sky-bound palaces of the Reigning Citadel, nor from the ships of Armada that drifted through cloud and storm like steel-winged leviathans. He was born with his feet in the dirt, with dust in his hair and smoke in his lungs.

And yet, she chose him.

Why?

We have turned the question over in our many hands, examined it from every angle, held it to the light like glass, searching for the flaw that might reveal the truth.

Perhaps it was because he was rare.

Not in the way of a prince or a prodigy, not in the way of men who carve their names into history, but in something simpler, stranger. Duncan Gaffer was unshaped by magic. The Enchantment Engine, which turned the will of the world as effortlessly as the tide draws the sea, did not touch him. He walked through the Queen’s court like a man through fog—unmoved, unchanged, untouched.

He was the only thing in her world that was wholly his own.

A curiosity. A marvel. A danger.

At first, she must have watched him the way a scholar watches an aberration in an otherwise perfect theory. A strange little thing, this man with the weather-rough hands, this glassblower’s son, this diplomat’s unremarkable heir. She kept him close, not as a lover, but as a question. And when the answer did not come, she drew closer still.

Did he love her?

Love (in this case, We think) is a thing of want, of longing, of need. We cannot say whether he longed for her.

But we know this—when a man is studied long enough, he begins to turn his face toward the light that watches him. When a goddess leans close enough to share your very breath, who would not believe themselves chosen?

Perhaps he did love her. Perhaps he loved the way mortals love fire, knowing they will be burned, knowing they cannot resist.

But love was never the point upon which their relationship's leverage weighed.

The court believed his ascension was a gift, a rare indulgence from their Queen, a game played for her own amusement. A mortal crowned consort, a beautiful bauble to sit at her side, a whisper of scandal among the lords and ladies who had vied for her favor and lost.

They did not understand.

We do not believe Duncan ever thought himself her equal. But he was hers. And that, in the end, was enough to damn him.

Because Queen Serraphine was a creature of foresight. She saw the shape of things before they came to pass. She knew the inevitabilities of war, of politics, of history turning beneath her careful hands.

And she knew how Duncan’s story would end.

Did she love him?

It is a foolish question. But one we ask because love is worth hoping for, We think. Serraphine had no use for longing. Not in the way mortals name it. She was above longing, beyond need.

What could she have wanted from him? A consort? A pet? A momentary amusement?

No.

She did not take him out of absence, but out of certainty. She took him because she had already decided how it would end. Everyone sees the same variable: a series of moments shaped to end. An immortal who is capable of granting immortality takes a mortal to whom she will not grant eternal life.

And yet… she chose him anyway.

And when his hair silvered, when his hands trembled, when time carved lines into his face, she let him slip away. She could have given him without any apparent cost to herself. But she did not.

He begged.

We know this. We have found the scraps of his final words in the mouths of those who served her, in the whispers of those who listened at doors, in the trembling voice of a man who stood at his bedside and carried the memory like a wound.

Duncan Gaffer, who had never asked for power, who had never grasped for more than he was given, asked for one thing. And she refused him.

She let him die.

Some say it was cruel. A final punishment for daring to believe himself hers. Others say it was mercy, the only kindness she could grant him.

But we, who see the shape of things, ask another question.

Was it protection?

Because when war came, when fire fell from the sky and the world cracked beneath the weight of its own sins,

Duncan Gaffer was already gone. He did not see his homeland burned, did not hear his name cursed by the survivors, did not suffer the fate that awaited those who had once loved the Queen of the High Reaches.

She let him die.

But perhaps it was the only way to save him. As an eternal creature Ourself, it is a thing We may be closer to understanding than We would prefer to be comfortable with.

We have retold bits of his story. The pieces we are most interested in.

But do not mistake this for an ending.

Because when we look upon Cait, we see Duncan’s ghost.

A child forged in magic, a weapon repurposed, a soul cast loose from the fate that should have bound her.

Duncan was special because he was untouched by magic, pristinely exempt. Spells simply did not interact with the soul or body. He was literally immune to the charms of a Goddess of Charm.

Cait is special because she is both powerful and child-like, untouched by ambition, pristinely naive. She is immune to self-destruction, and able to endure the crushing weight of despair.

Given the circumstances of their journeys and their interactions with Serraphine, We deem their lives to be two parts of a great equation that Serraphine seemed to think solved.

Both of them were chosen. Both of them were left to die. Both of them were freed.

So we ask, as we have always asked:

Did the Queen know?

Did she shape Cait’s fate as she shaped Duncan’s? Did she see the ruin she would bring and decide, in some quiet moment, to grant her the only gift she had ever given?

# Chapter 5: An Aubade of The Waking of a Goddess

The Queen does not wake. She is arisen.

A ceremony unto itself, a moment stretched thin with reverence. The palace does not mark time for her—it waits, breathless, for her to decide that morning may begin.

They come like mist, a hundred attendants in pale veils, hands empty until they are needed. Their presence is measured in heartbeats, their movements in whispers. They do not announce themselves. They do not speak. They do not make mistakes.

They warm the world before she rises.

The bellows continue, rhythmic, slow.

She already knows.

Their pain is not for her.

It is for them.

# Chapter 6: A Nocturne of Niel Benton

Three hundred mornings passed. Three hundred Ritus Obsequium—the ritual of stillness performed in reverent silence as the Queen rose. Each day identical, each day immaculate, each day marked by her absence of change. And during those days, Niel Benton worked.

The Lustre had gone dark and quiet for a time. She floated high above the world, unseen by the city below—her hull silent, her engines asleep, her watch kept only by the stars. There, in the hush of altitude and secrecy, he refined her. Tweaked and tuned, calibrated and corrected. Cait grew beneath his hands—not taller, not broader, but finer. Smarter. Stranger. He did not mark the days by clocks or candles. He marked them by her.

And now she stood at the helm.

Niel Benton had never been a beautiful man, but he had spent his life in pursuit of beauty.

His mother used to say he had the look of a bent nail—stubby and crooked, useful in a way but always second to something finer. His face was thick-featured, his nose too broad, set poorly after a break that had never been mended right. His eyes, sharp and restless, sat deep beneath a heavy brow, as if they were constantly searching for the flaw in what they saw.

But his clothes? His clothes could make a man forget.

His coat was Armada’s finest, indigo silk shot through with threads of gold so fine they glowed in candlelight. His cravat, stiff and perfect, was pinned with a blue gem the size of a baby’s fist, a stone cut so exquisitely that the Queen herself might have glanced twice. His boots were high, polished to a black so deep they drank the light.

And yet, despite all this, despite the wealth draped over his unremarkable form, he was not the most beautiful thing on this ship.

That honor belonged to her.

Cait.

She stood at the helm, the dying light painting her form in dusky gold. One hand rested lightly on the wheel, the other folded behind her at the small of her back—a human habit, something she had taken from somewhere, from someone, something he had not built into her. He was not sure where she had learned it. That irritated him.

“Unclench your hand.”

She obeyed without hesitation, fingers loosening with a grace that seemed as if they had never been tight at all.

Effortless.

That was what unnerved him. She was effortless now, in ways she had not been when he first wound her into being. When he had shaped her, sculpted her, bent the raw potential of her into a frame that pleased the eye, into lines and curves that would make a Queen pause. When he had adjusted her step a fraction of an inch to make her hips move just so. When he had smoothed the lacquer of her porcelain face until it reflected the world with a soft, flawless glow.

She had been effort, once. His effort.

Now she moved as if she wasn't a machine made by man.

His pride darkened slightly at the thought.

Below, the Craterlands sprawled—wounded, still burning in some places, the remnants of the war visible even from here. But ahead, rising above the world, the Reigning Citadel gleamed. White stone, gold trim, silver towers that did not cast shadows.

The Queen was waiting.

Niel’s jaw tightened.

No—worse. She made it look expected.

As if it were simply the place she was meant to stand. As if the wheel had been waiting for her hands.

Niel had designed her with presence in mind—every detail made to draw the eye, to be admired. She was exquisite.

And she was not looking at him.

Her eyes caught on the mirrored windows, set high and slanted toward the sky—not for light, but for reflection. Not for those below, but for something above. Something watching. Effortless.

“Beauty implies delicacy. Delicate flowers close before the rain even falls,” she said simply.

Not precisely.” Nodding to emphasize the word ‘Precisely’. ”Something between a person and a goddess. A step below me, but with one foot already planted in the higher order of things.” she mused on her own words for a moment and then continued: “Are you ready to take another step, Cait?"

The Queen’s steps circled the tree at the courtyard’s center, the leaves trembling as if in reverence. "You are not afraid," she repeated, as though testing the truth of it.

"No," Cait said, letting honesty settle into her voice. "I’m not."

A whisper of a smile touched Serraphine’s lips. She reached out, fingertips brushing one of the leaves.

"Then walk with me a while, Cait," she said, the layered harmony of her tone resonating in the quiet. "And let us see what else you are, or are not."

She wondered if Serraphine’s garments were woven from illusions, or if the woman herself bent light without meaning to.

It did not occur to Cait to refuse. She settled beside the Queen, her shoulders easing as she placed porcelain hands gently in her lap.

They watched the courtyard’s slender tree—a fragile thing crowned with pale blossoms that trembled in the breeze. For a moment, neither woman spoke, and the hush between them felt alive.

“You are so different from what I’ve seen.” Serraphine’s gaze drifted to the court’s silent ranks. “It was Bast’s vision that sharpened my sight. It is from that secret glimpse I recognize you.”

The Queen’s silvery eyes flicked sideways. “Does it beat for anyone?”

A faint hint of a smile curved Cait’s lips. “I’m still deciding.”

Cait lifted the fallen petal, holding it to the moonlight. “I think that’s because I don’t have the years others do, but I have… knowledge, maybe. A sense that there is more than I was built to know.”

Serraphine’s expression shifted, curiosity sparking in her pale eyes.

“Tell me something about yourself that your maker never taught you.”

Cait turned the petal between her fingers. “I like how the wind feels against my face, especially when we’re flying. The first time I took the helm of the Lustre, I realized… the sky is so big, so full of places we can’t see. But I belonged there. Maybe because it doesn’t have walls.”

The Queen studied her, silent for a moment. “You feel you belong among open skies rather than rooms of marble and gold.”

“Yes. Rooms are meant to hold things still. In the sky, I’m always changing.” Cait’s voice dipped into a hush, a note of wonder seeping through. “Yet here I am, in rooms of marble and gold, speaking to you. And somehow that feels like change, too.”

Serraphine touched a hand to her own robe, smoothing a shimmering fold. “I have lived beyond mortal time, yet I rarely encounter anything new.” She inclined her head, a gesture both regal and oddly gentle. “Why do you think I brought you here?”

Cait answered without hesitation. “Because I’m not afraid to look at you.”

A subtle tension flickered over Serraphine’s features. “Am I so dire a sight?”

“You’re beautiful,” Cait said simply, “like the hush right before thunder. But thunder doesn’t scare me. It just… signals a storm.”

Silence settled between them, like a breath held by the night. The Queen’s fingers idly traced the stone bench, her gaze shifting to the trembling blossoms on the tree. When she spoke again, her voice was hushed with reflection.

“People call me immortal. A goddess. But the truth is… I am not immutable. I have moved beyond many desires that shape mortal minds. It has left me untroubled by mortal concerns, largely.”

The Queen faltered. Trying to seem pleased with herself, Lustre tugged at Cait’s mind with a theory.

“Untroubled. I think you say it as though you mean calm, but do you mean stagnant?” asked Cait, blinking with genuine curiosity.

A fleeting smile ghosted Serraphine’s lips. “You are the first in a long time to speak to me without trembling. Do you know what that means?”

“It means you might decide I’m dangerous,” Cait answered, setting the petal on her knee. “Or… interesting. Possibly both.”

Serraphine nodded, the chains behind her swaying as though echoing the movement. “Yes,” she murmured. “Both.”

Cait closed her hand around the petal, crushing it gently so the fragrance leaked into the air. “If you decide I’m dangerous… will you unmake me?”

The Queen’s answer was not immediate. She breathed in the crushed-petal scent, her eyes drifting half-shut. “If you were just a thing, perhaps. But you’ve told me you are more than that, and I believe you.”

Moonlight caught in Cait’s silver-glass hair, turning it into a halo of shifting reflections. “Thank you,” she whispered, though she wasn’t entirely sure what she was thanking Serraphine for.

The Queen rose, the filaments of her robe catching the moonlight in prismatic shades. She extended a hand. “Walk with me again tomorrow, Cait. We have so little time… it is unpleasant to rush, but necessary.”

Cait placed the crushed petal aside, the residual warmth of Serraphine’s hand lingering on her porcelain skin. Tonight, she let that warmth carry her.

Tonight, she let it carry her.

They left the courtyard in silence, and if either of them wondered about Niel or the war waiting beyond those marble halls, neither gave voice to it.

For now, they had words unspoken, questions only half-asked, and the quiet drama of moonlight and shifting petals to sustain them.

## Chapter 8: An Aubade of the Lady Caine

His cheeks reddened at the frankness, and he averted his gaze, refusing to deny it.

Her hand lifted, gently resting on his shoulder. The lightness of the touch felt more startling than any demand. “Have it again, with us. Let the Reigning Citadel forget you… while you sell your brilliance to those who will actually pay.”

Despite himself, he found the gesture not entirely unwelcome. It was so easy to be angry at the world, to nurse his grudges alone. But this woman, with her horns and her subtle fragrance, spoke to something raw inside him. A need to be recognized, desired… useful.

“The war ends soon,” he said, a note of resignation in his voice. “What’s left to grab?”

She smiled, cunning and dangerous all at once. “More than you think. Loot, relics, old magical devices. The edges of war are messy, and messy edges are where money is made. The Creatorlands won’t recover fully for a decade or more. They may never.” Her tone lowered. “Let the Queen have her puppet and the burden of a conquered country both. We have other uses for your mind.”

He sighed. “She took everything from me.”

“Then take something for yourself,” she whispered, leaning in, letting him catch the faint perfume that clung to her skin. “Anger can devour you, or it can devour your enemies. I suggest you let it fuel your next move.”

Niel closed his eyes, feeling his breathing grow shallow. After a moment, he squared his shoulders. “Fine. I’ll consider it.”

“That’s all I ask,” she purred, stepping back with a graceful pivot. “I’ll be in the city another day. Find me if you make up your mind before then.” She paused at the door, shooting him a sidelong glance. “Or if you want company.”

He didn’t reply. She slipped out, her escort falling in behind her as the door closed.

Alone once more, Niel found his fists clenched at his sides. He hated how easily she had read him, how she had used her looks and her words to unearth every grievance he’d tried to drown in rum. But a kernel of resolve burned in his gut.

The Queen wanted nothing more from him. Cait was a lost cause. But there might be something left to salvage—wealth, respect, or at least the feeling of being desired for his worth. Even if it came from a woman with horns and a clear penchant for manipulation.

For the first time since losing Cait, he felt a wicked sliver of possibility. It tasted like bitterness and ambition fused together, a mixture potent enough to pull him out of his stupor.

He reached for a fresh sheet of parchment, ignoring the empty bottles at his elbow. His mind was already spinning with calculations of how far the Lustre could go, how many men it could carry, what corners of the world it could exploit.

If the Reigning Citadel no longer valued him, perhaps Lady Caine would. For the right price. And maybe he’d find a way to make her pay too, in time.

He tapped his pen against the page, echoing her words: let anger fuel his next move, then let his pen rest. Lips curled into a grim smile. The war might be ending, but a new one had just begun—his war. And Niel Benton would not remain overlooked any longer.

## Chapter 13: A Nocturne of Marian, As played by Kaliope.

It is well known to the players that in the play Poor Duncan, the part of Marian is an invention of the author. She is an emotional foil to Duncan, the object of his thoughts. Because with out someone to love him back, there is no story worth writing. In truth, Kaliope knows it is her story, not Duncans’. A woman in unrequited love with the discarded refuse of a heart. It is a chance for the audience to pity Duncan. To compare the love of a mortal woman in all of it's futility and frustration as a virtue when pit against Serraphine's treatment of Duncan.

Kaliope enters the stage for the last time, her heartbeat drumming so intensely she half-expects the hollow boards beneath her feet to echo it back. Though the Fan and Flame retains its conventional trappings—rows of plush seating, gilded sconces, a medley of lanterns faintly humming with half-shadows—tonight the assembled nobles project a different tenor altogether.

They do not shift in their seats or glance sidelong at one another; even their inhalations seem tentative, as though grief has eroded any motivation to draw full breaths. An unmistakable tension lingers in the air, as if sorrow itself has acquired physical weight.

She inhales slowly, surrendering her personal identity in exchange for Marian’s. That transformation usually feels almost reassuring, but on this occasion, a crushing awareness settles on her shoulders—a sense that the world behind the footlights has grown more brittle. Off to the left stands an aged trunk, scuffed from repeated use, illuminated by the quivering glow of a solitary lantern. Thespin Vell—embodying Duncan—enters from the wings in his worn doublet, his gaze charged with a rawness that transcends theatrical pretension.

A hush envelops them like a tangible presence, coalescing into an oppressive, almost sentient force. Kaliope finds herself bracing for an onslaught of feeling:

DUNCAN (quiet, pleading): “You would leave me, Marian?”

The resonance of his voice threads through her nerves. She has rehearsed her forthcoming response countless times, yet tonight each syllable strains under the weight of a looming catastrophe. Schooling her features, she delivers Marian’s line, though it emerges with a tremor she cannot disguise:

MARIAN (Kaliope’s voice, wavering): “I can no longer linger in half-shadows, nor endure the precariousness of each stolen moment.”

Her statement hovers in the charged silence. Ordinarily, a flicker of reaction from the crowd—some whispered empathy or collective intake of breath—would comfort her, confirming that the watchers remain engaged. But now, the spectators stare ahead with a uniform vacancy, their expressions carved by a sadness so pervasive it eclipses curiosity. They did not come to be entertained, Kaliope discerns, but rather to watch something irrevocable unravel.

DUNCAN (voice trembling with sincerity): “If only I had the courage to sever my vow to the distant queen… But my vow is all that marks me as worthy in these eyes, is it not?”

His tone brims with heartbreak, as though he truly teeters on the edge of emotional collapse. Stepping nearer, he penetrates the boundary between them, albeit superficially. She usually counters such intensity by gauging the audience’s energy—hoping to ground herself in their tacit encouragement or sympathetic hush. Tonight, the hush devours every nuance. There is no gentle exhalation of pity, no faint rustling of empathy. Only an unrelenting stillness, as if the entire hall collectively endures a funeral vigil.

MARIAN: “Worth lies not in a vow made to ice and echo.”

Her fingertips graze his cheek, registering the warmth of his skin in stark contrast to the chilling aura that pervades the auditorium. It is an ocean in which no lifeboat can sail.

DUNCAN: “Love me, or pity me, but do not vanish without a word.”

A pang constricts her chest. His plea, delivered so plainly, highlights the hush that overtakes them. This is no mere absence of sound; it’s an unspoken dirge. Peering into the rows, Kaliope takes in faces riddled with a grief that surpasses the boundaries of the script. They seem terror struck. Is this how an audience surrenders its hope? she wondered. Have they gathered here for one final ceremony of resignation?

MARIAN (Kaliope, voice trembling at the edges): “I cannot remain in a tragedy of unspoken truths. Tis a farce I cannot bare. For in the baring tis no burden under which we share a direction. I strain to move forward where you strain to remain. Farewell, dear friend for forward I move in all final haste lest my tears bind me as your fears bind you.”

She utters the line, each syllable pressing down with definitive finality, as if an unseen gate has definitively slammed shut. The stage lights flood the scene with a harsh illumination that feels accusatory. She notices a flicker of resignation flutter across Duncan’s eyes—resignation that mirrors her own. In that exchanged look, they both acknowledge that no fervent improvisation or heartfelt entreaty can redirect the course of this story. The sorrow in the seats has seized the narrative entirely.

Collecting Marian’s trunk, she slips into the recesses of the backstage, relinquishing the character Duncan to that solitary space of stage. He delivers his final soliloquies. The curtain’s descent is a relief, offering no semblance of celebratory closure. It is a long minute while the cast waits. Slowly, as if coming out of shock, the audience's applause begins.

With a long, quavering exhalation, Kaliope allows the hush to flood over her as well. She envisions, for a fleeting second, what it would have been like if the applause never came. But she dismisses the notion almost instantly: You predict a resurrection of the heart of an audience. You can only pray it beats again. And beat it did. Relief unknotted the cast.

At last, she sets down Marian’s trunk in a dimly lit corner. Even the ache in her chest feels futile, overshadowed by a sorrow she cannot define.

Outside, a faint rattle of chains signals the stage lights being extinguished. She can all but taste the relieved tension seeping through the walls. They remain out there, an audience that will want to express their thanks, to shake hands and to declare it to be ‘just what they needed’. With a weary turn, Kaliope heads deeper into the labyrinth behind the curtains. Only tomorrow's performance remained. Not for the first time, she was glad their run of Poor Duncan would be at an end.

## Chapter 23: An Interlude of Thaddeus King.

To her, it was thrilling. "Giddyup." She whispered in abject joy beyond all reason.

Kaliope let out a breath, rolling her shoulders once more, feeling the tension ease just slightly. The city of Severen awaited them, its walls still distant, its fate uncertain. But for now, the road stretched ahead, and the morning was still theirs.

A monolith of charred stone and impossible geometry, it had not been built but had instead descended from the heavens with the weight of a final judgment. A line of soldiers stood at the city gates, their postures rigid bored, their hands resting idly on weapons that had already earned their keep. Kaliope and Alan stood together in the press of bodies, their posture carefully measured. Now, it was their turn.

The female Orc barely glanced up. “Name?”

Alan cleared his throat. “Alan Vauxhall.”

“Origin?”

A pause. The wrong words could damn him. “Serraphine.”

The pen hesitated, then scratched across parchment. “Trade?”

“Tinker.”

A long pause. The soldier standing beside the clerk, a hard-faced man with the look of someone who had no patience for liars, raked his gaze over Alan. His eyes lingered on Alan’s hands—calloused, scarred, but not the hands of a common scrap-worker.

“Papers?”

Alan shook his head.

The soldier exhaled through his nose. “Five days.” The asylum pass was stamped with a sharp crack and shoved toward him.

Kaliope stepped forward, voice smooth as silk. “Kaliope Moray.”

“Origin?”

“Lanton.” A lie, effortless.

“Trade?”

“Scrivener.”

Another pause. The soldier’s gaze lingered on her, longer than it had on Alan. Cambrian features, striking and unmistakable—silver-and-pink hair barely concealed by the hood, curling horns shadowed beneath the cloth.

“I don’t like scriveners. Scriveners are forgers. You’re not going to forge gate passes or work receipts, are you?”

Kaliope didn’t flinch, didn’t blink. Instead, she met the soldier’s gaze with a slow, deliberate smile. “Only if you pay me first.”

A pause, then a snort—half amusement, half resignation. The soldier shook his head, turning to the clerk. A begrudging nod. The stamp fell, another pass slid across the table. "Yes. I pay. I pay for people who want forged papers. You turn them in. You get paid. But if you get caught. I'll toss you off the walls myself."

The soldier considered her, then turned to the clerk. A begrudging nod. The stamp fell, another pass slid across the table.

Then came Cait.

She sat atop the wagon, silent, hands gloved, hood drawn low. Kaliope gestured toward her with an easy flick of her fingers.

“Our driver. A bit touched in the head. Had an injury when she was young.”

The clerk raised an eyebrow but said nothing, jotting something onto the record. The soldier seemed satisfied.

And then—Nail passed gas. Loudly.

The sound was wet, rippling like a stone skipped across a still pond. A moment of silence followed.

And Cait, a flawless portrait of stillness and restraint until now, burst into laughter.

It was bright, sharp, utterly delighted. Not restrained. Not something a mute girl could manage.

The soldier’s attention snapped back to her. Alan felt his stomach drop. Kaliope stiffened.

Cait clamped a hand over her mouth, shoulders shaking.

Kaliope recovered fast. She let out a breath through her nose, shook her head as if embarrassed, and muttered, “Poor thing. She still finds farts funny.”

A pause.

Then the soldier—this man of discipline, of hard stares and inked records—exhaled through his nose, looking Cait over one last time.

He muttered something under his breath, a curse or maybe a prayer, then waved them through.

They rolled past the gate, past the weary clerks and the silent sentries, past the waiting throng of the displaced.

Beyond the walls, Severen sprawled before them—a city of marble and shadow, canals that gleamed in the midday light, streets threaded with desperation and quiet menace.

The tower of High Reaches loomed at the city's heart, an ugly thorn piercing the sky.

They were inside.

For five days, at least, they were free.

## Chapter 26: A Nocturne of Tiny Wizards.

## Chapter 27: An Aubade of the Dark Tower

The scent of spiced coffee curled through the air, rich and complex, laced with the faintest notes of citrus and clove. A rare import, freshly arrived from the harbor that morning. Kehris had taken the time to prepare it herself—not out of necessity, but as a ritual, a quiet assertion of control before the day's demands began their slow, inevitable creep toward chaos.

The Black Tower loomed over Severen like a judge, its sheer black stone an alien mark upon the city’s skyline. Unlike the intricate spires of the High Citadel or the drifting expanse of Armada, this was a fortress of occupation—rooted, unyielding, and absolute. The interior bore the unmistakable craftsmanship of Oligard, the mountain that defies the sky. Where the High Citadel rose in open grandeur and Armada thrived in its lawless expanse, Oligard remained enclosed, its life unfolding in vast, torchlit caverns where the echoes of hammers and the murmur of industry never ceased. The Black Tower reflected that ethos—walls of seamless steel, deep-set runes pulsing faintly with protective magic, gold fixtures placed with the measured weight of tradition rather than ostentation. This was Kehris' domain, and she did not tolerate disorder within it.

From the highest chamber, Kehris surveyed her guests. The chamber’s high windows, narrow as the slits of a helm, framed the cold morning light, casting stark lines across the polished stone floor. Below, the city of Severen simmered, its outward beauty barely masking the fractures beneath. The canals shimmered with reflected dawn, but in the alleyways, in the cramped refugee quarters, unrest coiled, waiting for an excuse to unfurl.

His bulk cast long shadows against the stone floor, his stance that of a man who had never needed to make space for anyone. It docked.

Three figures stepped onto the skybridge. One with a limp.

They weren’t challenged. They weren’t logged.

They disappeared inside.

Vane’s jaw flexed. He eased back into the shadows.

The Lustre wasn’t a ship of passage. It was a \*\*garrison.\*\*

And someone had bet their life that no one would notice.

The punch came from behind. No footstep, no warning—just the crack of knuckle against skull and the tilt of Vane's balance as his vision staggered sideways. He hit the stones, rolled, and came up with his forearm already priming another shot.

King stood at the edge of the alley, coat open, horns backlit by the flame-gutter of a trash fire burning low against the canal wall. His fists were still loose.

"Kehris might pay double if I bring you in alive," he said.

Vane flexed his jaw. Pain buzzed in his teeth. "Then you should’ve aimed better."

They clashed without ceremony. No introductions. No grandstand.

King moved like a landslide—momentum, mass, and unstoppable trajectory. Vane ducked under the first blow, countered with a snap-kick to the inside knee. King grunted, turned with it, and hammered a backfist into Vane’s shoulder. Vane rolled with it, shifted his stance, and lunged with a short punch to the ribs.

King didn’t budge.

Another flurry—Vane went for the neck, King caught him by the forearm and slammed him against a brick wall. Vane’s boots scraped the cobblestone as he twisted free, planting both hands against King’s chest and kicking off to regain space.

They were panting now. Chest heaving. Trash fire warping the light, painting the alley in flickering orange and soot.

Vane’s grapple fired.

The cable snapped out, caught King’s left horn mid-lunge. With a grunt, Vane yanked hard. The tension slammed King sideways into a gas-lamp post with a solid \*clang\*. The post bent, sparks coughing from the mantle as King ricocheted off it.

He stumbled a step, then turned back toward Vane, breathing like a bull driven too long, blood bright against one nostril.

They came together again.

King lifted Vane clean off the ground and drove him into the cobbles. Vane’s back hit stone, augments flaring to disperse the shock. He drove his elbow into King’s flank, rolled, kicked hard. King dropped to one knee—then surged up, driving his horns forward. Vane dodged left, narrowly avoiding the gore.

Blows landed. Some blocked. Some not.

A knee to the thigh. A punch to the solar plexus. A headbutt. Vane’s vision burst white.

They broke. Breathed. Circled.

Vane spit blood. King wiped his jaw.

Then again.

Vane’s foot caught King’s shin—low, brutal, meant to deaden the leg. King roared and swung wide. Vane ducked under, grabbed the collar, tried to pivot—but King shifted his weight and threw Vane bodily into the side of a canal stanchion. The impact rang.

Vane rose slow. Wiped sweat. Reset his shoulder with a wrenching snap.

Still standing.

King’s chest was heaving. His knuckles split. One horn glinted where the firelight kissed the soot.

They came at each other again.

Not clean. Not clever.

Just \*\*still fighting.\*\*

Until eventually they weren’t. Not because someone landed a final blow. Because both men hit the ground and didn’t rise. Not right away.

They lay there in the grit and smoke, chest to cobblestone, blinking up at nothing. The trash fire crackled a few feet off, painting the alley in red and shadow.

King rolled first, one arm folded behind his head, the other dragging across his mouth to wipe the blood away.

"You could just outbid her, you know," he said.

Vane didn’t answer. Breath still ragged. He turned his head enough to see the minotaur’s silhouette lean into the light.

"Kehris isn’t paying me nearly enough to do this twice," King went on. "But I figure you might have something more interesting on offer."

Vane’s voice came low. "Like what."

King shrugged, slow. "That’s what I’m asking. You’ve got plans. I’ve got ears. Might be we both walk away richer. Might be there’s something you want to buy."

Vane pushed himself up to one elbow. His ribs ached. His left leg was slow to follow.

"You ambush me. Try to sell me to the highest bidder. Then start talking contracts?"

"It’s Severen," King said. "That’s foreplay."

They sat there another moment. Long enough for the fire to crackle dry.

Then, without shaking hands, they both got to their feet.

"Two days," King said. "Gilded Net. It’s a fish joint. Dockside. Come hungry or come careful. I’ll have a partner for you to meet."

Vane arched an eyebrow.

"Lady Caine."

"You’ll like her," King added. "Or you won’t. Doesn’t really matter. But she’s the reason the Blades aren’t breaking your spine right now."

"Charming."

"Bring charm. It’ll be a rare night."

## Chapter 33 – A Nocturne of inkblots.

Kaliope stood alone on the stage with a pen clamped between her teeth and a sheet of paper curled in her fist. One lantern burned in a repurposed footlight socket, casting a single uneven cone across the dust-washed boards. The chairs out front were shadows. The balconies slept like stone mouths. The only sound was Cait shifting in her sleep somewhere in the wings, a card slipping loose from the top of her fragile monument.

She paced, barefoot, her steps soft but firm, as if she were daring the old stage to remember her weight. The paper crinkled as she gripped it tighter, then crouched with a mutter—half-line, half-insult—and scrawled something brief on the back of a receipt. She squinted. Tore it in half. Let the pieces flutter.

Ten rats emerged from the wings as if drawn by the sound of paper tearing. They filed into a soft crescent at the edge of the light. Each wore scraps: braided twine, blue ribbon, a bit of lace, foil twisted into the shape of a hat. They sat close, not performing, not curious—just present.

Kaliope glanced over without surprise, only a dry lift of her brow. She swept her hair off her cheek.

"Didn’t realize the balcony was sold out," she said.

No answer. One rat adjusted its robe with the deliberation of someone who cared how they were seen.

Kaliope dropped into a cross-legged sprawl. Her spine cracked on the way down. She scribbled something new. Tore it, slower this time. Watched the pieces fall like snow.

One of the rats cleared its throat. Or tried to.

"You’re early," Kaliope said without looking.

"We’re always early," said the group—slightly staggered, but practiced.

She smirked. Almost.

"Thought you liked shadows better."

"Shadows don’t talk back."

She tilted her head, amused, then serious. "You ever seen a curtain fall wrong?"

The rats stayed still.

"It sounds like a body dropping. The crowd holds their breath like it’s waiting for someone to scream. But no one does. They just clap. Like that’s how it ends."

She reached back and unpinned her shawl. Let it slip beside her notebook with a whisper. One of the rats tilted its head. Her blouse had risen slightly. A scatter of pale starburst scars crept along her side. Faint in the half-light, but distinct.

"Do you still ache?" one asked.

Kaliope stilled. Her hand drifted to her hem. She started to pull it down. Then stopped.

"No," she said. Her voice was quiet. Not wounded. Measured. "Not there. Not anymore."

One of the rats leaned forward, the embroidery on its little cloak catching the lantern light.

"They don’t look like pain."

Kaliope exhaled through her nose. Pushed hair behind her ear with a quick flick. "That’s because you’re not staring."

"They’re not the part people stare at," the group said. The tone had softened.

Kaliope leaned back on her elbows, her fingers curled into the grain of the stage.

"It’s not the scars," she said. "It’s what people decide they mean."

A rat climbed onto her knee. Waited there like it had all the time in the world.

She stroked its back with two fingers. Gentle. Thoughtless.

"Do you want to know the line?"

The rats nodded, all together. A small motion, reverent.

She sat upright. Shoulders square. Took a breath.

> “I told them I was her. And none of them noticed. Not even the priest. Not even her father. So what does that say about the girl I replaced?”

Silence followed. Not strained. Not broken. Just silence.

One rat adjusted its robes.

Kaliope’s mouth curved—just slightly.

"It’s stupid," she said. "Too obvious."

"It’s something."

She looked down at her notebook, flipped it face-down like it had seen too much.

"I’m calling it *Mirabelle*."

The rats didn’t move. One scratched behind its ear. Another smoothed the line of its cloak.

Kaliope eased down flat onto the stage. The boards were cool and uneven under her shoulder blades.

The rats stayed beside her.

She closed her eyes.

"I hope she gets away with it," she said.

This time, not one of them spoke.

After a long stretch of quiet, one of the rats tugged gently on the edge of Kaliope’s sleeve.

"You like him," they said. Not accusing. Just observant.

She didn’t open her eyes. "You’ll need to be more specific."

"Alan."

Kaliope snorted softly. "Alan. Yes. He does have a knack for trying to fix things that don’t stay fixed."

Another rat chimed in, its voice nearly a whisper. "He worries about you."

She opened her eyes now, turning her head just enough to meet their gaze.

"He worries about everyone. That’s what makes him miserable and useful in equal measure."

"You lean toward him," said another. "When you sit. When you’re hurt. Even when you argue."

Kaliope pressed the back of her hand to her forehead. "You’re remarkably nosy for creatures without pockets."

One of them raised their hands and then showed that his little wizard robe did in fact have pockets.  
Kaliope gave a brief giggle and slapped the flat of her hand against her forehead in the pale moon light. She sat for a moment looking at Lost from under raised eyebrows, giving him a flat look with flushed cheeks.

"He’s good," she said finally. "Good in a way that’s hard to look at for too long. Makes me feel like I’ve run off with an absentminded professor at times."

They didn’t answer that. Not with words. Just a faint nod that showed they understood.

– Several days later.

## Chapter 34 An Aubade of Opportunists at a Seafood Brunch.

The Gilded Net smelled like brine and lemon oil. A ship's galley trying to pass as a dining room. Nets hung from the ceiling more for namesake than decor, and every candle was cradled in an old oyster shell. It was the kind of place that existed out of habit rather than success.

Vane sat alone in a booth near the back, posture easy, coat slung open to show he wasn’t hiding a weapon. The only movement was his eyes—tracking the door, the angles of reflection in the glassware, the slow curve of the tide under the dock.

King arrived first. He filled the space like he owned it. Bruises still visible along his jaw, his gait just uneven enough to mark where Vane had landed a good one.

“You look better vertical,” Vane said.

“You look better unarmed,” King replied, sliding into the booth.

“I’m always armed. Just not always rude about it.”

The smile King gave wasn’t unfriendly. But it wasn’t warm, either.

They sat in silence for a moment. King gestured for wine. Vane didn’t bother.

“I’m not here to finish that fight,” King said.

“Good,” Vane answered. “Because you lost.”

Before King could reply, The Lady Caine appeared—quiet, as if conjured. She wore dark slate, gloves tucked beneath one elbow, and the kind of grace that made the room look like it had dressed to meet her. She took the last seat like it was always meant to be hers.

“Shall we be civilized?” she said.

“That depends,” Vane said. “Do you mean tactful, or just efficient?”

Caine smiled. “Is there a difference?”

King poured the wine. Three glasses. One untouched.

“You’re trying to build something here,” King said. “In Severen. In what’s left of it.”

“I am.”

“And we’re trying to survive it,” Caine added. “Different trajectories. Compatible needs.”

Vane leaned back. “You’re defecting.”

“Call it a transfer of confidence,” Caine said. “The Queen is consolidating. Making herself less mortal. People like us—mercenary, memorable—we’re not in the script anymore.”

“She’s cutting off the hands that bled for her,” King said.

“She’s choosing symbols,” Caine said. “We chose ships.”

Vane looked at her carefully. “And now you’re choosing me.”

“No,” Caine said. “We’re offering you the next line in your own legend. A chance to turn your people into more than rumors.”

She slid a slip of paper onto the table. It didn’t slide far—just far enough.

“A Flaming Blades depot. Soft target. Well-placed. Let your people hit it. Make it loud. Let the city see. Let the fear grow teeth.”

“And what do you get?”

“A ripple,” said Caine. “One small wave in the wrong direction, and even palaces start to list.”

Vane didn’t touch the paper. “You want instability.”

“We want freedom,” King said.

Vane’s gaze stayed steady. “You want to burn something down.”

Caine’s smile was unreadable. “Let’s say we want to stop singing the same anthem.”

He studied both of them. “You’re playing a long game. That’s fine. But I don’t volunteer to be someone else’s myth.”

“We don’t need a volunteer,” King said. “Just someone who likes the spotlight enough to stay in it.”

Vane reached for the paper. Folded it once, then again.

“If I do this, it’s my people at risk. I don’t give them over easily.”

“They’ll survive,” Caine said. “And if they don’t, you’ll be remembered louder.”

Vane slid the folded paper into his coat. “I don’t like being used.”

“You like being feared,” Caine said.

He didn’t answer.

But he didn’t leave.

The deal was not spoken. But it was made.

And Vane, for the first time in days, wasn’t sure if he’d just gained control or given it away.

## Chapter 35 – An Interlude of Molly

The rain made the Copper Lantern feel smaller, like the walls had folded in just enough to hold the heat. Lanternlight pooled in mugs, boots steamed near the hearth, and the back room card table was losing another coin toss to someone with too many eyes and not enough scruples.

Molly moved through it like a fish through tight water—elbowing a keg into place, catching a falling spoon with her foot, and refolding a napkin that hadn’t earned its freedom. Hob-built and barrel-sturdy, she ran her bar with the kind of grace that had nothing to do with elegance and everything to do with remembering who owed what.

Near the window, three Enders played cards and muttered about job prospects. Nothing loud. Just the usual barstool tension: someone saying Vane had work lined up, someone else claiming the list was closed, and a third just grunting into his cider. Molly didn’t interrupt. She didn’t need to.

The rat at the bar had heard them, anyway.

Dressed in a stitched cravat and sipping a row of thimble wines—dry red, oily gold, and something she suspected was cough syrup—the little bastard had already gone still. One paw hovered over the rim, motionless.

Molly slid behind the bar and leaned down.

“You paused. That means something. Spill.”

The rat blinked.

“Don’t give me that. I’ve poured for spies with looser mouths than yours. What’d you hear?”

The rat picked up its wine, swirled it delicately, and didn’t drink.

Molly exhaled through her nose. “Vane’s poking at something, isn’t he? There’s talk, but not the kind that spreads. The kind that waits.”

The rat tilted its head.

Molly poured herself half a finger of warm gin and didn’t bother with a glass.

“Don’t suppose your lot has told the Trident crew.”

The rat licked its whiskers.

“They’d want to know,” she said. “Probably. Alan’d say no. Kaliope would laugh and say yes and mean no until she changed her mind. Cait…”

She trailed off.

The rat finished its wine.

“She’s the one I’m worried about,” Molly said quietly. “Not because she can’t handle herself. Because she doesn’t know when she’s meant to.”

The card game in the corner got loud. Someone accused someone else of counting in base twelve. A chair scraped. A bottle fell. Molly didn’t look.

“I’ll tell them,” she said. "Soon. But not yet. Let them have one more day where it’s just repairs and reheated stew."

The rat bowed its head. No promise. Just understanding.

Molly downed the rest of her gin.

Outside, the rain picked up. Inside, no one noticed.

Not yet.

## Chapter 36 – A Nocturne of Porcelain and Glue

The Jewelbox creaked in its sleep. One of the rigging lines ticked against a beam like it had something to say but forgot the words. Cait crouched near the edge of the stage with a stripped gear assembly in her lap and a bolt clamped delicately between two fingers.

Alan was nearby, working on a cracked joist. He made little sounds when he worked—grunts of effort, the sigh he let out every time something aligned. He’d rewrapped his knuckles from a splinter earlier. She hadn’t told him he did that more when Kaliope wasn’t around.

She rotated the bolt slowly, listening to how the thread caught and skipped.

“You don’t sleep much,” he said.

She didn’t look up. “I try. But I don’t get better at it.”

Alan muttered something under his breath. It might have been sympathetic.

The light was dim—lantern flame in a glass shell, warm enough to cast shadows that moved on their own. Cait liked this time of night. The theater was quieter when it wasn’t trying to impress anyone.

She adjusted her grip. Something pulled sharply in the joint of her index finger. She opened her hand. There—a fracture, just below the second knuckle. A fine line, too perfect to be accidental.

Alan had noticed. He was already moving toward her.

“That’s new,” he said.

“It’s not deep.”

He crouched beside her. Looked at the crack, then at her face.

“Does it hurt?”

“I don’t think so,” she said. “But it’s worse than it was.”

Alan set down his tool belt and reached out. “Let me try something.”

She didn’t move.

His fingers were rough—heat-callused and uneven. They smelled like metal polish and wood glue. He pressed two against the fracture and closed his eyes. A hum, low and almost shy, moved between them. Her arm didn’t glow. Her body didn’t shift. But the fracture closed like a mouth deciding not to speak.

Cait stared at her hand.

“You didn’t fuse the seam.”

“Didn’t need to.”

“It worked.”

Alan exhaled. “Good.”

Cait rotated the joint. The movement was smooth now. There was a pause in her chest. Not breath—just something that felt like it.

“I didn’t ask you to fix it,” she said.

“You didn’t stop me.”

She tilted her head. “That counts?”

He shrugged. “Sometimes.”

She looked down at the bolt in her other hand. Turned it once. Then set it aside.

“Do you ever wish someone could fix you?”

He hesitated. “Not like that.”

Cait nodded. She didn’t quite understand, but she liked the sound of it.

They worked a while longer. She passed him things before he asked. He told her when to hold and when to let go. His voice changed slightly between the two.

When they stopped, she sat beside him. Matched his posture. Folded her hands the way he folded his. Looked at the ceiling, even though she could see through it.

“You fixed me,” she said.

He shook his head. “I repaired a part.”

She blinked. “What’s the difference?”

He didn’t answer right away.

“I don’t know,” he admitted.

That, at least, felt like truth.

A draft caught the ropes above. One tapped lightly against the beam.

Cait listened. It didn’t sound like applause.

It sounded like waiting.

## Chapter 37 – An Interlude of Sparks and Casting Shadows

The Jewelbox was loud with energy that didn’t know where to go. Hammered nails rang sharper than they needed to. Laughter spilled in uneven bursts. Someone rehearsed a death scene loud enough to spook the front row of rats. Kaliope watched it all from a paint-streaked stool near the edge of the stage, a stack of script pages in her lap and a coffee gone tepid beside her boot.

There was no system yet—just momentum. Bodies moved through tasks like dancers who hadn’t agreed on choreography. One Ender tried to tie back a curtain and ripped the loop in the process. Another tripped over a prop barrel marked "quarantine rations" and apologized to the room. Kaliope didn’t stop them. She liked chaos better when it was hopeful.

“From the top!” she called, snapping the red pencil like it was a baton.

A cluster of voices shuffled into position. One read from a half-memorized line, something about saltwater and wedding bands. Another mumbled their cues while watching a girl with shin-augments climb the flyrail like it owed her money.

Kaliope turned her attention to Cait—silent, observant, standing with one hand on the pulleys like she could feel the scene through the ropes. Alan walked past with a canvas stretcher under one arm and an expression that suggested the theater might be a patient he wasn’t ready to lose.

Molly leaned against the doorframe. “Looks like a militia with better diction.”

Kaliope didn’t turn. “Or actors with worse intentions.”

“I count five weapons onstage and two backstage.”

“I’m calling that balance.”

Lost’s rats were lining the edge of the orchestra pit like ushers. One had a chalked-up prompt card. Another held a miniature lantern.

Kaliope rose.

“Nessa, you’re up.”

Nessa Corth stepped into the center light. She was steady now—less stranger, more center of gravity. The Cambrian cloak moved with her like it belonged.

Kaliope flipped to Scene III and handed her the page.

“Misrabelle. The dressing table.”

Nessa didn’t ask which part. She found her breath and let the line fall like a blade.

> “You want the truth, Jove? Then let’s have it.  
> You had a wife. Clairabelle. And now I’m meant to fold myself into her gowns, stand in her light, and call it union. You’ve buried a woman and ordered another in her shape. Do you want me to smile like she did? Moan like she did? Die quietly like she did?”
>
> “Let me be clear.  
> I am not your echo.  
> I am not your revision.  
> And I will not share this stage with ghosts in borrowed pews.”

The silence that followed wasn’t theatrical. It was the kind people held when they remembered too late that they were in the presence of something already happening.

Kaliope waited until the air loosened.

“Again,” she said. “Let it crack this time. He’s not your audience—he’s your mirror.”

Nessa breathed, nodded, and went again.

From the wings, Alan’s tools stayed untouched. Cait, still and sharpened, watched like she might step in if the lights failed.

Alan came to sit beside Kaliope as the echoes of Nessa’s second reading faded into the velvet hush. He passed her a wrapped parcel—half a sandwich, still warm, with bread charred at the edges and layered with something smoked and sharp.

She unwrapped it wordlessly and tore off a corner. He took the other half and leaned his shoulder just close enough to say, *We’re allowed to rest for a breath.*

They watched the stage in silence for a beat. Nessa stepped back into the dark, still holding the weight of the lines like a new coat that hadn’t quite settled on her shoulders.

“Could work,” Alan said.

Kaliope nodded. “More than could. If she sticks.”

He chewed. “You sure about letting Enders play roles? Some of them aren’t… subtle.”

“She doesn’t need to be subtle. She needs to be believed.”

He passed her the crust. She took it.

“And when the show ends?” he asked.

Kaliope wiped her fingers on her thigh and glanced sideways at him. “Then we strike the set, clean the blood, and call it rehearsal for whatever comes next.”

He huffed through his nose. Not quite a laugh.

Kaliope leaned forward, watching the way Nessa stood off to the side now, eyes scanning the page even though her lines were done. “You think I’m reckless.”

“I think you’re not scared enough,” Alan said. Then: “But I think that’s why it might work.”

They sat together as the next reader stepped into place, neither of them saying anything more. The sandwich was gone. The lights held steady.

Somewhere above them, a stage weight shifted and settled.

The Jewelbox breathed in.

Vane arrived with the weather—quiet, prepared, and too well-timed to be accidental. The side entrance gave him a clean view of the house and the stage beyond, and Molly was already beside him, carrying a second mug of something black and bitter.

"You're early," she said, not quite surprised.

"Curious," Vane replied, accepting the drink. "It’s not every day I get to see my coin turn into poetry."

Molly raised a brow. "You're expecting verse?"

"I’ll settle for receipts," he said, watching the activity unfold with a banker’s stillness. “But if it sings, I won’t complain.”

They stood back from the center aisle, flanked by stacked chairs and half-covered crates of unused props. Onstage, two Enders dragged a backdrop into place with more enthusiasm than coordination. A woman with oil-smudged sleeves adjusted a lantern rig with the casual authority of someone who didn’t wait for permission.

Nessa passed through the space again, still in costume, still holding something in the air around her that hadn’t dissipated since her reading. Kaliope walked beside her, one hand on her shoulder—not correcting, just anchoring.

“That one,” Vane said. “She lands well.”

“Used to file dock schedules,” Molly replied. “Now she’s got half the room holding their breath.”

Vane nodded. “Sometimes the trick isn’t becoming something new. It’s being allowed to show what you already were.”

His gaze slid past the footlights. Cait stood at the edge of the wing, adjusting a counterweight pulley with the care of someone who knew what it could do if it failed. She moved like architecture—all design and tension.

Molly followed his eyes.

“She’s not an asset,” she said quietly.

“Noted,” Vane replied, without blinking.

Molly sipped. “You’re not here to meddle?”

“I’m here to watch my loan stand on its own legs.”

“Lot of legs in this room,” she said. “Not all of them stable.”

He smiled, faint and unreadable. “That’s why I loan interest, not trust.”

A bell clanged backstage. Someone yelled a cue too early. Kaliope laughed—a sharp, delighted sound that cut through the noise like cymbals.

Vane’s face showed his approval.
