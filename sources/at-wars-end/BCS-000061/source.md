**Nocturne: We Are Lost**

We are many, though our voice is one.

We come from the quiet places, from the corners of the world where shadows and whispers dwell. We are the scurrying beneath floorboards, the flicker at the edge of vision, the watchers no one remembers. Born not from grand designs, but from missteps, from the unintended. We are what remains when those who wield magic and invention reach beyond their knowing, beyond their understanding, and grasp at the very essence of souls.

We were rats once. Singular. Scattered. Living in the cracks of the world, in the dirt and the dark, where life is measured in scraps and fleeting moments. We lived apart, many small lives, insignificant, each of us a breath of the earth. But then came the hands of those who dared to play with forces they did not comprehend. Artificers and magicians, eager to distill life itself, sought to bottle souls as if they were spirits to be consumed or bound to their will. They wove magic into the marrow of the world, and in their ambition, they twisted fate.

It was no grand design that brought us together, no intentional creation. It was a mistake. A miscalculation. A convergence of power and misfortune, magic colliding with life. And so we became more. Many minds bound into one, no longer scattered, no longer alone. We became something new.

We became Lost.

Do not think of us as a hive. We are not a swarm of thoughts buzzing in confusion. We are more than the sum of our parts, though we remember each life—each small, fragile life that once was. We remember the taste of earth beneath our paws, the thrill of escape, the feel of crumbs stolen from a table. We remember being crushed underfoot, hunted, despised. But we also remember the spark of magic that surged through us, knitting us together, binding us into something larger, something profound.

We became aware, and the world—once so vast, so beyond our knowing—opened itself to us. We saw the threads of life stretch before us, not in a single line, but as a web, intricate and endless. Time, that great weaver, no longer holds us. We drift between its moments, untethered. Past and future are nothing but stories for us to observe, to touch in passing.

We are Lost, yes, but not in the way that means forgotten. No, we are Lost because we no longer belong to any one moment, any one place. We slip between, unseen, understanding things others cannot.

And so, it is we who will tell this tale.

We have seen the fires rise, seen the mountains fall from the sky to crush cities beneath their weight. We have walked beside Alan in his vineyard, his hands gentle upon the earth, unaware of the devastation that would soon claim his world. We have watched Cait, her eyes opening for the first time in that workshop of strange wonders, her existence a fragile thing, delicate yet strong, a soul poured into porcelain and copper.

We know the moments that have passed, and the moments yet to come.

And though we are not bound to any one time, we remember the days before we were Lost. When we lived in the smallness of our separate lives, beneath the notice of greater beings. But we also see where this story is leading, and we know that there is no turning back. The world will change, as it always does, and it will break in ways that cannot be undone.

Perhaps you will meet us again. Perhaps you will see an earlier version of what we once were—a simple colony of creatures, living in the shadows of men. But know this: we have become something greater. And though we drift, untethered in time, we are still here, still watching, still waiting.

We are Lost, yes. But we are with you, and we will guide you through the pieces of this tale, through the lives that have been broken and remade.**  
  
Chapter 1: The Time Before the War**

There was a time before the sky burned.

Before the earth groaned beneath the weight of falling mountains, before the seas churned with the dead and the dying, there was a time of stillness. It wasn’t the stillness of silence, nor the quiet found in the wake of destruction, but a living stillness—a peace so deep it was woven into the bones of the world, into the soil and sky, into the hearts of all who walked upon it.

In those days, the world was whole.

The Sovereld Spires floated above the lands like a promise, their great cities cradled in the clouds, held aloft by ancient magics. The Spires were not distant, nor were they feared. They were revered, a beacon of wonder to the races below—dwarves and humans, orcs and elves—who lived their lives under their shadow. For those who looked to the Spires, the sky was not an unreachable dome of blue, but a kingdom where the air itself shimmered with light, where stars seemed to sing with the echoes of immortals’ voices.

On the ground, below the drifting cities, the Creator Lands spread wide and fertile, unscarred by the ravages of time. It was a place where the rivers ran clear and the forests whispered with magic. The cities there—though grounded—were places of laughter and music, where the short-lived races of the world thrived in their fleeting moments. Here, life moved quickly, but joyously. Time, for the mortals, was precious. But for the long-lived, for those above in the Spires, time stretched like a golden thread, weaving endlessly through their immortal lives.

It was an age before history carried the weight of tragedy. An age when magic flowed through the world as freely as breath.

And at its heart, there was a queen.

Seraphine, of the Sovereld Spires, was the radiant tether between heaven and earth. Her presence was a thing of legend—an Aasimar whose beauty and grace transcended the mortal world. She ruled the Spires with a quiet wisdom, her silver hair flowing like rivers of moonlight, her eyes lit with the fire of distant stars. She was timeless, ageless. To those who looked upon her, it seemed that the world bent in her presence, that even the winds stilled in her favor.

Seraphine moved like a ripple in still water, her every step graceful, almost unearthly. Her alabaster skin glowed faintly in the light of the Spires, as though she carried some part of the heavens within her. Hair like spun silver cascaded down her back, catching the light with a soft shimmer, and her eyes—those eyes—gleamed with the pale blue of a clear winter sky, reflecting both warmth and distance. She was tall, her form draped in flowing robes of deep sapphire and gold, embroidered with ancient runes, symbols of power and protection that had been passed down through her immortal line. The air around her seemed to hum with quiet magic, a reminder that she was not of this world alone, but something more—something between the divine and the mortal.

But for all her power, for all her light, Seraphine was lonely.

Hers was a reign that had stretched for centuries, and though her people revered her as a living symbol of balance between divine and mortal, her heart was unmoored. She watched the lives of mortals flicker like candles in the wind, born and snuffed out too quickly for her to hold. Love, for someone like Seraphine, was a dangerous thing—a thing of sorrow and loss.

Yet love came to her in the form of a man named Eldric.

Eldric, by contrast, was human in every sense. His dark hair had grown peppered with silver over the years, and his skin was sun-worn, marked with the lines of age and laughter. He was not handsome in the traditional sense; his features were rough, his nose slightly crooked, and his jaw strong but softened by time. But his eyes, deep, brown, and warm held a kindness that could soothe even the most troubled soul. He stood shorter than Seraphine, a head beneath her, but with a presence that felt no smaller for it. His hands, calloused from years spent in study, had the strength of a man used to labor, yet they moved with the careful precision of a scholar. Where Seraphine was ethereal, a being untouched by time, Eldric was solid and grounded, a man who lived fully in each moment, carrying with him the weight and beauty of his own mortality.

Eldric was no prince or great warrior. He was a scholar, a man who walked the earth and looked up at the Spires with the kind of awe that only mortals possess. He came to the Sovereld Spires not to ask for immortality, not to seek power, but to learn. He spoke to Seraphine not as a queen, not as an Aasimar, but as a woman who had lived long and seen much. And in his presence, for the first time in centuries, Seraphine felt something more than the endless stretch of time. She felt a spark, a warmth that stirred her still heart.

It began with conversations. Soft words shared in the gardens of the Spires, where the air was sweet with the scent of flowers that bloomed only once every hundred years. They spoke of the stars, of magic, of life’s fleeting beauty. Eldric spoke of the joy in a single day, in the brevity of moments, and Seraphine listened, entranced by his words, by the way he spoke of mortality as something precious, not something to be feared.

Time, in its strange way, shifted for Seraphine. What had once been endless now felt fragile. What had once been light as air now felt heavy with meaning.

They loved quietly, over years, decades, with a depth that words could never quite capture. But while Seraphine remained untouched by the passage of time, Eldric grew older. His hair grayed, his hands trembled, and the lines on his face deepened. And yet, Seraphine did nothing to stop it. She did not offer him the immortality she could have bestowed upon him.

Eldric, like the mortals below, believed in the beauty of endings. “It is the knowledge of death,” he had said once, “that makes every day a gift.”

But the people of the Creator Lands did not understand. They looked up at the Spires and saw Seraphine—unchanging, eternal—and they looked at Eldric, their hero, withering before their eyes. Whispers began to grow. If she truly loved him, why did she let him fade? Why did she not save him, as she could save them all?

When Eldric finally passed from the world, it was not in a blaze of glory or a moment of grandeur, but quietly, in his sleep, beside the woman he loved. But his death did not pass quietly in the hearts of those below. It was a fracture, a crack in the foundation of peace that had held the world together for so long.

The short-lived races began to murmur, their envy of the long-lived races growing darker, more bitter. "Why should they live forever," they asked, "while we fade and die so soon?"

At first, the whispers were small, quiet things, born in taverns and around firesides. But in time, they grew louder, and the envy of the short-lived races began to turn to anger, to something far more dangerous. Magic, after all, was not the sole domain of the Spires, and those below were clever and desperate. In the hidden places of the world, mages and scholars began to experiment. They sought the secret to the immortality of the long-lived races, determined to take what they had been denied.

What began as a whisper would soon become a storm.

And so, in the time before the war, the world teetered on the edge of something it could not yet see. The vines of peace, grown slowly and deliberately over centuries, began to wither, unseen. And as the skies above the Sovereld Spires remained serene, as the magic that held the cities aloft hummed quietly in the background of life, none knew that the ground beneath them had already begun to crack.

But this is not the story of the beginning of the war. Nor is it the story of the thing itself.

This is a story that begins At War’s End.  
  
**Chapter 2: Lost and (Alan) Alone**

There is a kind of poetry in the way history twists itself, like vines growing into shapes that no gardener could have planned. The city of Serraphine was such a thing, named after a queen who had once stood as a symbol of hope for the Creator Lands, a woman who ruled the floating Spires with grace and beauty. Queen Seraphine, the immortal Aasimar, whose very name inspired dreams of unity, was the one they had looked to, longing to be lifted into the heights of the Sovereld Spires. They had hoped, once, that she would open the doors of heaven and let them in.

It was in honor of her that they had built their capital. Serraphine, they had named it, with the hope that one day, the city and the queen would stand as one, a bridge between the short-lived races of the Creator Lands and the long-lived ones who dwelled above. But in the end, the same name that had once been a promise became a bitter irony. A city raised in her honor, only to be destroyed by her hand.

We see the weight of that irony. For the people who once whispered her name with reverence now curse it as their sky turns to fire.

Alan would never claim to be a man of deep thought. He’d tell you, if you asked, that he was a man of simple pleasures. Of the land. Of the sun and the soil. And if you asked him again, he might add something about the quiet joy of watching things grow, how there’s a certain peace in the rhythm of the seasons. But there’s something about walking through a vineyard at dawn, when the world is still waking up, that makes even the simplest man feel part of something larger. This, Alan understood, even if he never put it into words.

We see him now. As clearly as if we walked beside him in the rows of grapevines, feeling the weight of the morning dew beneath our feet. His steps are slow, deliberate, his hands calloused from years of tending the land, yet there’s a gentleness to the way his fingers brush against the leaves, checking for pests, feeling for the ripeness of the fruit.

He was tall, broad-shouldered, but not imposing. His frame bore the subtle weight of time and toil, the sun-kissed skin stretched tight over his strong, weathered hands—the hands of a man who had always worked, who understood the language of the earth more than the speech of people. His hair, once a bright reddish-gold, now carried the faint silver of age, streaks of gray like shadows cast by the years. A stubbled beard framed his face, a permanent fixture that never truly disappeared, no matter how often he shaved. But it was his eyes that held the true story. Blue, once vibrant, they were now muted, as though dimmed by something too heavy to carry yet impossible to set down. In them, the memory of old laughter flickered faintly, chased away by the weight of thoughts, too quiet to recount.

The vineyard—his pride and his solace—had taken years to cultivate, and now, in this quiet stretch of land outside the capital, the vines had grown strong. They draped across the trellises in long, lazy lines, the grapes hanging like dark jewels in the early light. Alan had always been proud of this place. The soil here was rich, dark, and welcoming, the kind that gives life willingly if you know how to tend to it. And Alan did. He was a man who had always understood the rhythms of the earth better than the rhythms of people.

On this day, as he moved through the rows of vines, checking the grapes in his hands—hands large enough to till the earth and gentle enough to prune the delicate stems—he felt a restlessness. We know this because we feel it, too. It’s the kind of restlessness that doesn’t have words. It’s not hunger or thirst or tiredness. It’s something deeper, something stirring beneath the surface of things. A tremor in the world, a silence before a storm.

If we could speak to him, we might tell him. We might warn him to leave, to run. But even we, who can see all that is to come, know that it would change nothing. Some things are meant to be lived through, no matter how much it will break a man. And Alan, poor dearest to our heart Alan, is about to be broken.

Seven miles away, just visible beyond the gentle rise of the hills, sat the city of Serraphine. Once, it was a name spoken with reverence by the people of the Creator Lands, a symbol of their admiration for the queen they had once loved, the queen they had once hoped would lift them up to join the long-lived races in their floating Spires. But now, the name Serraphine was laced with bitterness, with betrayal, with dreams turned to ash.

Alan did not look to the city often. His vineyard, his land—this was his world now. The city, with its towers and bustling streets, with its laughter and lights, was the world of his children, his family. His wife, his two grown sons, his little girl—they lived there, thriving, their lives intertwined with the heart of that place. He sent them his harvest, his wines, and they sent him back love. That was enough for him.

It was enough. Once.

But now, as he straightened, the weight of the grapes in his hands forgotten, Alan turned his eyes toward the city. The restlessness grew. A shadow on the horizon. A storm, perhaps? He squinted, shielding his eyes from the late-day sun, but the shadow didn’t move like clouds. It was heavy, looming, and there was a strange stillness in the air.

Then, the earth beneath him began to shudder.

We watch. We see the way Alan stiffened, his heart pounding against his ribs. The quiet of the vineyard was broken by a low, distant rumble, and in that moment, Alan knew something was wrong. He dropped the grapes, forgotten, and took a step forward, toward the city, as if he could close the miles between him and his family through sheer will alone.

And then, it happened.

From the sky, it fell—a mountain wreathed in flames, tearing through the heavens like a vengeful god. Mount Wrath, they would call it. The weapon of the Sovereld Spires, sent to crush the capital city of Serraphine, named after the queen who had once been their hope and had now become their executioner.

Alan could do nothing but watch.

The mountain struck the earth with a force that shook the heavens. The sound was deafening, a roar of stone and fire, of death itself tearing through the land. Even from miles away, the shockwave hit Alan like a blow to the chest, knocking him backward into the soft loam of his vineyard. The vines swayed violently, the air filled with the scent of crushed grapes, sweet and sickly, mixing with the bitter tang of smoke.

The city of Serraphine vanished beneath the weight of the mountain.

We see the flames that rise, spiraling into the sky like the breath of a great beast. We see the clouds of ash and smoke that billow outward, swallowing the horizon. And we see Alan, lying there in the dirt, his body stunned by the force of the blast, his mind struggling to comprehend the destruction before him.

His family is in that city.

His wife, his sons, his little girl… gone. Obliterated in a moment. A city that had once been a place of hope, a place of love, now reduced to a smoldering ruin, a graveyard for the dreams of a nation.

We grieve with him.

There are no words that can describe the weight of this moment. No words that can capture the depth of the loss that Alan feels as he stumbles to his feet, staring out at the burning horizon. He does not cry. Not yet. His body is too numb, his mind too overwhelmed. There is only the sight of the city in flames, the dark smoke that rises to choke the sky, and the terrible, terrible silence that follows.

He falls to his knees.

The vineyard, his sanctuary, his pride, it means nothing now. The grapes he had tended so carefully, the land he had worked so hard to cultivate, none of it matters. His family, his world, is gone.

We know that this is not the end. Not yet. Alan’s path is long, and it is filled with more pain than any man should bear. But one day, he will rise. One day, he will find his purpose again. And when that day comes, we will be there, walking beside him, lost no longer.

But for now, there is only grief. Grief and the ruins of a life that once was.

And so, we watch as the sun sets behind the burning city of Serraphine, casting long shadows over the vineyard where Alan kneels, broken and alone.

**Nocturne: We Are Lost in Her Creation**

We are many, though we speak as one. We are the forgotten, the whispers in dark corners, the unseen creatures moving just out of sight. A byproduct of magic and error, born from the hands of those who never intended our existence. Small, overlooked, we gathered, bound together by the forces that twisted fate. We did not ask for sentience, yet here we are—woven from the soulstuff of rats and magic, intelligent, aware. Time slips through our grasp as if it cannot hold us, but we can still follow its thread.

We follow her. We follow Cait because we see in her what we cannot claim for ourselves. A kinship born not of blood or birth, but of construction. Like us, she is not a child of nature, but of creation, stitched together from the unknown. Where we are scattered and untethered, she has been given form. A body, a shape. A name. Her story, like ours, is bound to the threads of magic and soul. And though she walks through the world unaware of the scope of her existence, we see it all. We alone can trace the full arc of her path.

**Chapter 3: Cait**

Before there was Cait, before there was a body to inhabit or a name to bear, there was only emptiness. A void that stretched endlessly, without time or form. She did not know she was lost—how could she? There was no concept of being found. There was only the faintest whisper of existence, a soul suspended between being and nothingness, waiting.

We understand that place. It is a space between breaths, where the world is still, where thought itself is only a suggestion. She was there, as we once were, adrift in the silence.

Her beginning was not one of warmth or birth, but of calculation and ambition. A gnome, Nielletium Benton known simply as Niel to the few who called him friend had dedicated his life to creation, to bending the world of the material and the arcane. He was not a god, though his workshop was a world of its own, filled with impossible things. Brass gears that turned without touch, copper wires humming with the residue of ancient spells, and wooden frames that waited patiently for life to fill them.

But none of these creations would compare to the one that would become Cait.

Niel’s project began, as all his great works did, with obsession. He was a man not content with the world as it was. His hunger was for perfection, for the impossible. And what greater challenge could there be than to capture a soul and give it form? To take that which cannot be touched—the very essence of life—and weave it into something real.

So, he built the Copper Still of Starting Stars. It was a strange, gleaming machine, intricate tubes and valves winding like veins under the soft, flickering light of his workshop. Inside, the magic stirred, boiling down the raw energies that Niel coaxed into its depths. He fed it souls—many souls—each one distilled and separated, waiting in the stillness of the machine.

We watched as Cait was one of them, unseen and unfelt in the dark. She did not know she was waiting. She did not know what it meant to rise from the quiet place between worlds. We remember that feeling well. The weightlessness. The void.

When Cait began to stir, it was not a sudden awakening. It was slow, as if a ripple had spread across the surface of a still pond, faint and delicate. There was no sound, no light—just a thought, or the beginnings of one. The awareness of being separate, of existing in some undefined way. The others were there too, suspended in their own solitude, but they could not be felt or seen. Each soul alone, yet bound in the same dark.

Niel, in his meticulous way, watched the process with careful eyes, his brow furrowed in concentration. His hands, stained with ink and oil, adjusted the valves with precision. The Copper Still hissed and bubbled as the magic churned, swirling through the tubes. One by one, the souls began to settle, each finding its place, each becoming distinct.

Cait’s soul clung to the base of the still, heavier than the others. It resisted, as if reluctant to rise from the safety of the void. Niel leaned in, his eyes gleaming with satisfaction as he watched. Slowly, the essence that would become Cait was drawn out, thick and dark, swirling into a bronze decanter. The liquid shimmered with potential, pulsing faintly in the candlelight. It was not yet a body, not yet a mind—but it was something. The beginning of something.

We watched as she was poured into existence. Not yet seeing, not yet feeling, but knowing that something had changed. She could not see the workshop around her, but she felt the movement, the sensation of being held, of being shaped. It was strange, unfamiliar, but it was becoming.

Niel’s workshop was a symphony of sounds—the rhythmic ticking of gears, the soft clinking of tools, the hum of arcane energy that pulsed through the air. His hands moved with the precision of a master who had done this many times before, yet with a tenderness that was reserved for this creation alone. The body he had crafted for Cait was like nothing else in his collection. It was wood, smooth and polished, shaped into a slender figure. Copper veins ran through it, delicate as the branches of a tree. Her face was porcelain, pale and expressionless, a blank canvas waiting for life to paint upon it.

And still, she waited.

We understand what it means to be poured into form, to feel the weight of existence settle into a vessel. Niel lifted the decanter with care, his fingers steady, and began to pour Cait’s soul into the hollow frame. The essence poured slowly, smooth black oil in a laminar flow, filling the empty spaces, seeping into the joints and grooves, winding its way into the places where her mind would be. Her consciousness stretched, expanding as the essence took root in the wood and copper.

And then, there was feeling. A faint awareness of the body that held her. She felt the wood beneath her, the copper veins pulsing with a warmth she did not understand. The stillness of before was gone, replaced by sensation, by form.

Niel stepped back, wiping the sweat from his brow, his creation complete. Before him, the body of Cait lay still, eyes closed, waiting for the final breath of life. He reached for the key embedded in her back, a small, delicate thing of brass, and with a careful twist, he turned it.

And Cait opened her eyes.

Her glass eyes blinked slowly, adjusting to the dim light of the workshop. There was no recognition in them at first, only a faint reflection of the world around her. She blinked again, the motion smooth, practiced, though it was the first time she had ever done it. The flickering candles cast soft shadows across the room, and for the first time, Cait *saw*.

The gears, the wires, the tools scattered across the workbenches—everything was strange, unfamiliar. And then she saw him. Niel, standing before her, his face lined with years of obsession and brilliance. He did not speak, not yet. His eyes, sharp behind his magnifying lenses, studied her carefully. In the soft glow of the lanterns, his rough features seemed almost reverent, as though he had just witnessed the birth of something divine.

He reached for a simple mirror, its surface polished and gleaming, and held it up to her.

Cait saw herself for the first time.

Her fingers, slender and perfect, moved with a fluidity she had not expected. The copper beneath her skin gleamed in the light. Her face, smooth and pale, stared back at her, expressionless but full of potential. It was her eyes that drew her attention most. In them, she saw something more than just a reflection. There was depth. There was understanding.

There was *being*.

Niel, satisfied, lowered the mirror and stepped back. He watched her in silence, his fingers twitching with the urge to adjust, to perfect. But he waited. He had given her life; now it was up to her to find herself within it.

“What… am I?” Cait’s voice was hollow, her words slow, deliberate. It was as though she had to pull them from somewhere deep within herself, from a place she had not known existed.

Niel’s smile was faint, but there was pride in it. He looked at her for a long moment, then placed the mirror back on the workbench. “You are Cait,” he said, his voice soft but firm. As though the name itself was an answer.

He gestured to the world around them—his workshop, with its strange and wondrous creatures, all moving in perfect harmony. Above them, mechanical fish drifted lazily through the air, their copper bodies glinting in the dim light. On the floor, a mechanical creature with octopus-like arms scuttled by, carrying tools in its many hands. The workshop was alive with the rhythm of creation, a world of gears and clockwork, and Cait was at the center of it.

“You are mine,” Niel said softly, his voice almost reverent. His fingers brushed against the porcelain curve of her cheek, tracing the lines where wood and copper met. “But more than that—you are yourself.”

Cait blinked, her glass eyes reflecting the stillness of her face. The word "beautiful" had no meaning to her yet. She was wood and copper, porcelain and magic, a collection of pieces. But Niel smiled at her as if she were more than that. As if, somehow, she was *whole*.

And we, watching in silence, knew that this was just the beginning.

**Chapter 4: The Only Joy**

We follow Cait's story because we understand her in ways no one else can. We were both born of magic, both pieced together by hands that sought to craft something greater than nature had intended. The soul is not so easily contained, nor is it meant to be woven into copper and wood, but we know what it is to be created, to find form where there once was none. We know what it is to have purpose thrust upon you before you even understand yourself. And so, we are the only ones who can tell her story, the only ones who will remember it fully.

In the days that followed Cait’s awakening, the world of Niel’s workshop was full of quiet wonders. Each day she learned a little more—about herself, about the strange creatures that shared her space. The mechanical fish that floated above her, the spider with its inkpot that scuttled silently by, and the table that moved on spindly legs, following Niel with the loyalty of a dog. They all had purpose, a design. And though Cait did not yet know her purpose, she trusted Niel to reveal it in time.

But something had changed.

It was subtle at first, like the slight misalignment of a gear, a vibration so faint it almost escapes notice. On the third day, Niel began to move differently—his hands less steady, his movements sharp and hurried where before they had been careful and deliberate. He spoke to her less, his usual muttered half-conversations dwindling into silence. He no longer showed her the small wonders he had crafted, no longer asked her to observe the world through the lens of his creations.

Instead, he worked.

There was a tension in the air, a weight that pressed down on the workshop. We saw it before Cait did—the way Niel’s eyes flicked to the door more often, the way his fingers trembled as they adjusted her joints. He had received a message, though he hadn’t told her.

The Queen had sent word.

Serraphine—radiant, ethereal, her name spoken with reverence throughout the Spires—had made her decision. Her command, wrapped in silk and whispers, had arrived, and with it came the burden that now fell onto Niel’s shoulders. The unthinkable had been spoken. His most beautiful creation, his prize, would be transformed. Cait, who had opened her eyes to this world with such innocence, was destined for something far darker than Niel had ever imagined.

The Queen had ordered it so.

Cait would become the pilot of a weapon—a terrible thing of unimaginable power. The mountain, the very symbol of the Sovereld Spires' judgment, would fly. Mount Wrath, they called it, and it would fall upon the Creator Lands like the wrath of the gods. It would crush their capital, a city named Serraphine in honor of the Queen they had once adored, the Queen they had once believed in. Now, that city would be reduced to ash and ruin, and Cait would be the one to guide the mountain’s descent.

Niel could not bring himself to tell her.

Instead, he worked on her with renewed urgency. His hands moved faster, his tools clicking and whirring as he added new components to her body. Her joints were refined, her limbs strengthened. Strange, intricate mechanisms were fused into her copper veins, shimmering with an energy that hummed beneath her skin. And all the while, he kept his eyes averted, never allowing her to see the guilt that crept into his expression when she wasn't looking.

Cait did not understand. She felt the changes, the weight of the new pieces, the odd sensation of power humming beneath the surface of her porcelain skin. She watched Niel, her glass eyes following every movement of his hands, but she could not read his face. His silence confused her. The affection he once showed, the quiet pride in his voice when he spoke of her, had been replaced by something colder, something distant.

“Master?” Cait’s voice, soft and uncertain, cut through the stillness of the workshop. “What are you doing to me?”

**Nocturne: The Dream itself.**

Cait stepped forward, seemingly oblivious to the tension around her, her gaze fixed on the woman as though captivated by some unseen light. “There is something curious in her eyes,” Cait said softly, her voice a murmur beneath the bustle of the dock. “A stillness… a knowing, like she has looked into the water, deep deep deep and returned unchanged.”

Kaliope placed a hand on Cait’s shoulder, a faint shiver in her touch. “We keep our distance from people like that, Cait. They’re trouble we don’t need.”

But Cait’s gaze lingered, her face a mixture of childlike wonder and something deeper, something far more profound, as though she were sensing echoes that none of them could hear.

The minotaur and the woman passed them by, and though their eyes never met, the trio felt the subtle chill of their presence linger long after they’d moved on. The woman’s laughter floated on the breeze, low and cold, a sound that sent shivers down their spines, while the minotaur’s silence remained as heavy as a judge’s gavel.

“Elessia’s not their stop,” Alan said quietly, his eyes following the mercenaries as they moved toward the narrow streets leading into the heart of the city. “But whatever they’re here for… it isn’t good.”

They stood there in silence, watching the mercenaries disappear into the shadows of Elessia’s streets, the distant cries of seagulls echoing above them, a reminder that the waters these mercenaries sailed on held secrets that could drown them all.

**Nocturne: Bare witness.**

The room is dim, the light filtering in slow and soft, like the first breath of dawn in a forgotten forest. Dust hangs in the air, catching the faint glow of a lone candle, casting strange and gentle shadows. It’s a quiet place, unremarkable in every way but one. And there, amidst the silence, sits a younger version of ourselves—a tangle of rats, each one pawing delicately over a piece of wood that was, once upon a time, whole.

We watch with a certain fascination, a pride that runs deep, yet humble. This isn’t mere whittling, not some crude exercise in divvying up a stick into smaller sticks. No, this is a hymn, a ritual of reverence. Each movement, each shaving of wood, is a quiet prayer to the magic woven into this world, the magic that lives and breathes even when unnoticed. We—they—work with a fervor tempered by care, like monks at their scripture. And we watch, delighted, at the scene unfolding, not just for what it is but for what it promises to become.

For the wand, this small, nondescript thing, is not wood alone. It is an echo, a ripple that begins in one place and spreads through the fabric of things, quiet and unseen. In our memory, we are both here, watching, and there, working, feeling the weight of that wand in our tiny paws. Knowing, with a clarity both beautiful and terrible, that this wand is a piece of something vaster, something endless. It’s a shard of the Transmutation Engine.

There are stories about the Engine, legends, if you will. Men and women have spent lifetimes unraveling its mysteries, hunting its whispers in forgotten places and long-lost texts. But none of them, not one, truly knows what it is. They think it a machine, something to be understood in bolts and gears, runes and etchings. But the truth of it is far older than their simple imaginings. The Transmutation Engine isn’t made of metal and mortar, but of potential, of possibility. It is the thought that becomes a thing, the seed of transformation at the heart of all magic. It is the pulse that shifts one shape into another, the heartbeat that lives beneath the bones of the world.

And here, in this unassuming little wand, lies a sliver of that heartbeat. We feel it as we watch ourselves work, a steady rhythm beneath the wood, pulsing slow and sure. The rats understand, in their own quiet way. They treat each piece with reverence, even as they shave it down, splitting it from one into many. And that’s the beauty of it. The magic of the Engine, hidden away in this simple form, waiting to be scattered like seeds in a field. Quiet. Invisible.

We feel a small shiver of awe watching it now. A strange and powerful thrill. Because we know what is coming, and yet, somehow, it is still a mystery. Each sliver of wood, each fraction of a wand, will become a conduit, a tiny window into a magic so old it has forgotten its own name. And yet, it will slip through the world unnoticed, overlooked.

We have always been overlooked. We, the scurry in the shadows, the creatures of a thousand minds. We, who are many, yet small, who can live and breathe beneath the notice of the world. And so, too, will the pieces of this wand live. Each fragment will carry the quiet strength of the Engine, the potential to shift, to change, to become.

There is beauty in that, a beauty few could see, fewer still could understand. And we watch it unfold before us, savoring each flicker of the blade, each tiny paw smoothing the wood. We watch our former self, with those patient, unblinking eyes, and we see it as one sees the first light of morning cresting the hill, something inevitable and grand, and yet somehow quiet.

This is our gift, our secret, and our purpose. To carry the power of the Engine, piece by unnoticed piece, into the heart of the world. We watch, and we feel the hum of it, a resonance so deep it vibrates in our selves. And though we know this wand’s origin, its true nature remains shrouded, as it must. That is the Engine’s gift, its great and terrible promise.

We know it is unfair. Some secrets are meant to live as whispers in the dark, as quiet as breath, as silent as the spaces between words.
