Nocturne:

And as night falls, our path and our hopes return to the Copper Lantern, where Kaliope unpacks her violin again. Gerdie is there, moving between tables with quick, nimble steps, her eye on every patron, every drink, but her gaze softens each time it falls on Kaliope. When she passes, she offers a nod, a fleeting smile.

Kaliope meets her eye. “You don’t mind a few tunes tonight?”

“Mind?” Gerdie says, almost laughing, setting down a tankard with a practiced clink. “This place feels half-empty without them. Keep playing, love.”

And so Kaliope plays, and her music fills the room, gentle and rich, melding into the quiet conversations, drawing attention, if only for a moment.

Near the stage, we watch a goblin tapping his foot in time, his battered, patchwork coat barely hanging onto the seams, fabric so threadbare that its color is more memory than dye. His left leg, broken off at the knee, ends in an ivory limb—a piston of exposed metal, crude gears, and springs that catch on each other with a subtle, grating sound, like the creak of an old hinge. With every shift, the limb quakes faintly under his weight, yet he taps along with stubborn defiance, a rhythm kept as if in spite of the contraption’s own protest.

There was a warmth to that night we’ve rarely felt since—a warmth that drifted through us as we took our place on the edge of the table, pawed hands curled around a cup as if it held more than just an amber drink but the very essence of what it was to belong. We remember tilting it, carefully, a liquid swirl catching the firelight, and for a moment, as the drink tipped toward us, we felt we were in perfect command.

“Just a sip, Lost,” Allen advised, his grin that quiet, wry shape, full of things unsaid but understood. “Can’t have you toppling over.”

Kaliope’s gaze lingered, something soft beneath her smile. “I wonder,” she murmured, “if he knows what he’s in for?”

And, of course, we straightened, indignant. “We know perfectly well.” We nodded, attempting something solemn, like the elders we imagined ourselves to be. Allen’s chuckle was low, a warm rumble that felt like approval.

With that, we took a sip—just a sip—and felt a heat bloom on our tongue and slip down, curling in our belly like a gentle fire. But as if the drink had its own will, it tumbled further, spilling over our whiskers, each tiny drop glimmering in the firelight. We froze, caught in the act, looking up at Allen and Kaliope, both of them now wide-eyed and grinning.

Allen’s laughter was a deep, rolling sound, joined quickly by Kaliope’s softer, musical laugh, the kind of laugh that made a room feel lighter, as if all troubles had drifted to the edges, softened. We, still blinking, licked our paw, the warmth settling into us, filling every little self we are with a shared, comforting glow.

“We’re… warm,” we murmured, almost to ourselves, turning our paws over to watch them tremble with something new, something that went deeper than the drink.

“Oh, I think he’s glowing,” Kaliope said, her voice a gentle tease.

And indeed, there was a faint light to us, a shimmer that clung like a second skin. It was as though each of our small selves glowed, brightened by something we hadn’t known we could feel. We stretched our fingers, marveling, as a quiet awe spilled out from somewhere deep and wide, filling us until it felt we could almost be just one.

“All of you,” we murmured, lost in the sound of our own voice, “we’re all here together. Every bit of us feels… safe. Like we’re wrapped in blankets.”

Allen’s smile softened, and he rested a hand near us on the table, steady, his eyes warm. Kaliope leaned closer, a question in her eyes. “Tell us, then. What’s it feel like, Lost?”

“It’s… all of us,” we said, as if discovering it for the first time. “All of us warm and safe. As if we could almost be just… one.” A small, quiet breath escaped us as we leaned into the wonder of it, feeling every shimmer and flicker of the firelight reflected in our hands, feeling held in something bigger, something bright and close.

“Oh, he’s drunk,” Kaliope declared, amusement dancing in her eyes. “Look at him—lost in his own splendor.”

We lifted a paw, dignified, “We are… important,” we replied, though the words came softer, a bit slurred, falling into the silence that grew gentle around us, as if the very air understood.

And then, as these things tend to go, the moment tipped—just a little. The goblin near the stage tilted his head, intrigued, muttering with a grin, “Oh, look at the little prince, putting on airs.”

“Oh, no airs,” we said, as if speaking to an audience much larger than the few patrons in the room. “Simply a redistribution of wealth.” We lifted a small slice of bread from the table with a flick of magic, sending it floating over to the goblin in a stately procession. It dipped once, twice in greeting, before landing in his lap. He blinked, then burst out in laughter, clutching the bread as though it were the finest treasure.

“You’re making new friends, Lost,” Allen chuckled, raising his cup again in a quiet toast.

Near him, a man with a crooked grin leaned over to his companions, nudging his empty mug toward us. “Oi, little one, can you make \*this\* fly?”

We eyed the mug with all the gravitas we could muster and, with a flick of magic, lifted it slowly, unsteadily, in the air above the man’s head. The room fell quiet, laughter hushed as all eyes followed the mug’s path. It bobbed in the air, dipped to the side, and—just as it seemed it might stay aloft—plummeted with a clatter back to the table. The room erupted in applause, a round of thumping and laughter filling the air.

From the bar, Gerdie leaned over, eyebrows raised, her eyes sharp. “Well now, that’s a bit much for one night, little Lord Lost,” she murmured, with a smile that was both fond and final. We blinked up at her, aware suddenly of how warm we were, how everything seemed to shimmer and blur at the edges, but also… how this moment was held close, a memory already taking shape.

In the end, we returned to the table, settling onto the edge of Allen’s plate, watching the firelight, feeling each paw as if for the first time. There was a contented stillness in the room, like a song winding down, softening. Kaliope smiled, leaning in close, her hand resting just next to ours. We felt her warmth, and Allen’s presence, and something else—a sense of the Copper Lantern itself, holding us, safe within its glow.

We remember that night not for the drink, but for the warmth that spilled over and around us, a memory that would rest in our bones like something quiet and kind, something to call back to when shadows pressed in. In that little glow, in the laughter and the light, we were not many. We were one.

Kaliope, with a smirk of her own, shook her head with a smile.

“How about a story instead.”

Lost, feeling the room’s energy shift as Kaliope began to call on the Iron Heart, let the bread drop gently to his paws and settled in, his drunken amusement fading into quiet fascination as the room fell under Kaliope’s spell once more.

Kaliope moved slowly across the dimly lit stage, her gaze cast not at her audience but somewhere beyond, as though she were speaking not to those gathered before her but to a vast, silent assembly of ghosts. Her silhouette cut sharply against the shadows, and when she raised her hands, a faint shimmer filled the air—a hint of towers, rising like fragile bones into the darkness, a throne glinting faintly, and the faintest outline of a queen, her posture poised and unyielding. Kaliope paced, allowing the images to settle before she spoke, her voice low and rich, laden with a gravity that quieted every breath in the room.

"Do you know," she began softly, almost as if to herself, "what it is to love a thing too brilliant for hands to hold? They say Serraphine, queen of the Sovereld Spires, in her Citadel of High Reaches, was the embodiment of all that is perfect, all that endures. Her beauty—untouched by time, unmarred by mortal sorrow—made her a beacon, a flame to which the Craterlands flocked, hoping to warm their own lives in her light. She was loved… oh, how she was loved." Her lips curled, a trace of softness and longing in her expression, and she turned her head slightly, as if listening to some faint, mournful echo.

Lost, his curiosity piqued, drifted closer, his wide eyes following the line of sound as if it were a tangible thing. The faint glow of his magic dimmed, forgotten, as he settled in, rapt and still.

"And among her admirers," she continued, stepping slowly along the edge of the stage, her voice weaving a subtle, silken tension into the room, "was a king—a man whose heart knew no measure, whose love burned as fierce as any flame ever born of the Craterlands. He loved her wholly, without thought for himself, in the reckless, honest way only a mortal can. He would have torn the stars from the sky to lay them at her feet, his hands burnt and bloody, just to see her smile."

A soft, shimmering illusion took shape beside her: the figure of a man, youthful, upright, his face open and fearless, cast in a way that made every patron feel they knew him, that he was real and close. Kaliope let her eyes linger on him, and intimacy slipping into her gaze as she lifted a hand, almost as if she might reach out and touch the image of this earnest, tragic figure.

"But," she whispered, letting her fingers fall slowly, her voice breaking like a thread of silk, "time to we mortals born in the land below is a more cruel master than any other. The king, bound by birth and blood to earth, bore the years as we all do—with lines that marked his face, with silver creeping into his hair. And though he loved her just the same, her gaze… it grew colder, her glances sharper, like knives turned against the thing she could not bear to see. The man she’d once cherished, adored, began to look to her as something… lesser."

Her steps slowed, her voice gathering a strange, biting warmth, a bitter empathy. "One night, his strength all but spent, he knelt before her—yes, the king of the Craterlands, kneeling like a boy before a goddess, his trembling hands outstretched. He asked her, as a lover, as a husband… as the man who’d given her all he was, all he’d ever be. He asked her to share her secret, to grant him the eternity she wore so carelessly."

Kaliope turned sharply, her voice cutting through the stillness, raw yet measured. "And Serraphine looked upon him, this man who’d loved her beyond any human measure, and saw… not a partner. Not a king. Not even a man. She saw only his age, his weakness, and felt, in that cold and distant heart, the barest flicker of disgust."

The illusion of the queen shifted, casting her in harsh light, her face serene, untouched, while the king knelt before her, his figure now stooped, dim, like a candle flickering at the end of its wick. A murmur went through the room, an uneasy, dark understanding.

"‘You would have me chain myself,’ she said to him, her voice like steel, ‘to something that withers as swiftly as the grass. I will not bear such frailty beside me. You are… not fit to carry eternity.’" Kaliope’s voice lingered on those words, dripping with Serraphine’s coldness, her gaze darkening as she spoke, as if feeling the full weight of those words upon her own heart.

She let the silence settle, let the patrons feel the sheer cruelty of it, of the queen’s choice to let the thing she had once loved fall to ruin rather than tarnish her perfect, unchanging life. The illusion of the king faded slowly, as though the years were stripping him away, and Kaliope watched, her voice quiet and taut.

"He did not live long after that," she continued, almost a whisper now. "Though what he died of—whether sickness, age, or simply a heart broken beyond mending—none can say. She kept him near in those last days, watched him fail in silence, no tenderness in her gaze, only the faint, cold pity of one who no longer sees another soul but a shadow… a possession."

A hush fell, heavy and charged, and Kaliope looked out at the crowd, her gaze fierce, burning with a quiet defiance. "And so, the Craterlands looked upon her, this queen they had once adored, and they saw her for what she was—a creature of indifference, a queen willing to keep a man like a relic, to watch his years waste away like some experiment, never to grant him the kindness of her secret."

Kaliope took a step back, the images dissolving around her, leaving nothing but her and the memory of that cold, fatal disdain. "They turned their backs on her then, their love soured to loathing, their wonder twisted to scorn. They would never beg at her feet again, never plead for the light she held. They would take it themselves, by blood, by fire, if they had to. For what is love, what is eternity, if it leaves those you cherish to rot beside you, like a pet kept for amusement, a bauble to be tossed aside?"

Kaliope raised her violin and began to play “That Passing Light.” A simple hymn sung famously at the funeral of the King.

Kaliope’s voice joined the music, a sweet invitation to the room with the hum of the violin. Her words fell like drops into still water, carrying their own quiet weight:

"Beneath the stones, where shadows dwell,

We hold the flame that time won’t quell…"

She played on, the violin filling the spaces between her words, each note carrying the tune forward like a heartbeat. Allen glanced at her, caught by the way the melody seemed to deepen, drawing everyone closer to something unnamed but familiar.

We drew the napkin to ourselves, folding and refolding its edges with delicate care, our many thoughts settled into the rhythm of Kaliope’s music. It was not a mere scrap of cloth in that moment—it was a quiet tether, something solid to hold while the song swept through the Copper Lantern like a tide. We could not help but watch her, captivated by the way her melody curved through the air, each note seeming to shape the very room.

Around us, the patrons softened under her spell. Their gazes fell inward, shoulders loosening as if her tune had found some hidden knot within them and gently unraveled it. Burdens lifted, if only for a breath, their faces caught in an expression that hovered between sorrow and solace

The music swelled, subtle but sure, each note a pulse of strength that held no bravado, only the kind of courage that grows in silence. Kaliope’s bow moved faster, weaving in a hint of defiance, an iron thread in the melody.

Gerdie stood behind the bar, arms folded, her gaze softened as she watched. She saw the way her patrons leaned into the music, shoulders relaxing, eyes fixed on some distant thought or memory. A few exchanged glances, the kind that didn’t need words, something knowing and ancient passing between them.

Lost, perched near Allen now, let the folded napkin drift from his paws, his head tipping slightly, eyes half-closed, as though he, too, was carried somewhere else by the hymn.

Kaliope’s voice faded, leaving the violin to carry the final refrain, a tune that lingered in the space like a candle’s glow, each note a quiet promise. Allen felt the song settle into him, something warm and strong rising in his chest. It felt like an invitation—no, a reminder—that even grief could be softened, shaped into something that lasted.

“We are the embers, not yet ash,

A love that waits, a heart held fast…”

The final notes lingered, fragile as cobwebs in the still air, tracing the Copper Lantern’s edges before slipping quietly into silence. They didn’t fade so much as settle, seeping into every crack and corner, a warmth that refused to leave entirely. Around the room, eyes fluttered shut and heads bowed—not in prayer, but in something softer, an unspoken reverence.

Patrons sat suspended in the quiet, cradling the echo of the melody as though it were something tangible, something they could clutch to their chests and carry through the shadows of their lives. For a moment, no one moved, as if breaking the stillness might scatter the feeling, leave it irretrievable. They lingered, anchored by the music’s afterimage, held steady in its glow.

We remember that night, the heavy warmth of the room, the way everything seemed softer, blurred at the edges. We were young, barely more than children, our thoughts still bright and scattered like fireflies. The drink had settled in us, thickening our limbs, blurring our mind, and for the first time, we felt unsteady, like we were swaying on the edge of something vast and dark.

Kaliope’s story seeped into us, unfurling slowly, too big, too sharp, pressing against the inside of our ribs like it was remaking something in us. Her words carved out a new space we hadn’t known existed—a cold, hollow ache that was both strange and familiar. The story of the queen and her king, of love turned bitter and cruel, of something cherished left to ruin. It settled in us, heavy and unwelcome, and though we didn’t understand it fully, we knew it mattered. We knew it would follow us.

We watched her, our vision swimming softly, catching her eye just once. She looked at us—through us—with an expression that felt as old as stone, and in that gaze, we sensed the weight of truths that lay buried, of things unspoken. She looked away, leaving us adrift, her story lingering like smoke in the back of our mind, curling and settling in the empty spaces.

Around us, the crowd stirred, the warmth and noise of the room coming back in pieces, pulling at us to rejoin the world. But we stayed still, our fingertips brushing the rough wood of the table, grounding ourselves, holding onto that weight she’d left behind.

**Nocturne: The Dream itself.**

Allen is alone in a place that feels both familiar and strange. He stands in the vineyard, or what remains of it—the vines twisted, frozen in time, their leaves brittle like glass. The air is thick with silence, and though he can see the remnants of his life scattered around him, there is no emotion. No warmth.

Then, the light shifts. The vineyard bends and warps until it becomes a hallway, the walls made of glass, fragile and cracked, light shimmering through like liquid gold. At the far end of the hallway stands the *Dreamwalker*.

She is tall, her figure shimmering like moonlight caught on water. Her skin glows softly, almost translucent, and her eyes are vast, shifting with the colors of a distant nebula. Allen feels an instant pull toward her, but as he takes a step forward, the ground beneath his feet splinters like ice, and he stops, frozen.  
  
The Dreamwalker stood before him, a figure of alien beauty that defied the boundaries of the waking world. Her form was tall and lithe, a feminine silhouette draped in a flowing gown that shimmered with an ethereal glow. The fabric moved as if it were submerged in water, slow and languid, swirling around her in rippling currents that never quite touched the ground. It was a dress made of sadness, woven from strands of moonlight and shadow, each fold catching the dim light and refracting it in soft hues of blue and silver.

Her hair, a cascade of turquoise that tumbled down her back like a waterfall, was not still. It moved, not with the breeze of the room, but with something deeper—an unseen tide that swayed it, as though she was connected to the ocean itself. From the sides of her slender neck, strange, translucent tubes extended, rising with her every breath, pulsing faintly in rhythm with her presence. The tubes glowed faintly, as if they siphoned something from the air around them—drawing in the atmosphere of the dream, feeding on the unspoken sorrow that permeated the space.

Her skin was pale, almost translucent, but not cold. It gleamed like polished marble, touched with the faintest hint of turquoise under the skin, veins running delicately along the surface. Her fingers, long and elegant, moved with grace, as if every gesture had been carefully rehearsed in some forgotten language of loss. And then, her eyes—bright, burning orange—cut through the dimness, demanding attention. They were not the soft amber of the setting sun but the piercing blaze of a distant star. Her gaze was unblinking, sharp, and filled with a depth that felt like it could drown anyone who met it.

And yet, there was a strange comfort in that gaze. The sorrow she carried was not sharp or jagged—it was sweet, possessive in its quiet way, demanding to be felt but offering something in return. It called to the deep places inside Allen , to the hollow spaces where grief should have lived but had been locked away. Her sadness was not violent or forceful. It was a hand reaching out, pulling him toward her, telling him that it was okay to give in, to let go, to be consumed by it.

The longer her eyes lingered on him, the more he felt it. Her needs—so intertwined with his own—whispered to him, urging him to surrender, to let the walls inside him collapse and let her sorrow fill the void. There was an intimacy in it, a strange allure in her presence that was undeniable. She offered not the sharpness of regret but the softness of release, the quiet promise that within her sorrow, there was peace.

Her dress, floating in slow, hypnotic waves, seemed to reach for him, drawing him closer without a single word. She was beautiful, but it was the kind of beauty that hurt. It was the beauty of a memory long lost, of a love left behind, of everything that could have been. And in her presence, Allen felt as though he had always known her—as though her sorrow had always been there, waiting patiently for him to return to it.

She was sadness personified, not as something to be feared, but something that belonged to him, that had always belonged to him. And for as long as her bright, burning eyes were upon him, it was all he needed.

"Heavy," she whispers, though her lips do not move. The air itself carries her voice. The hallway bends, and with it, the weight on Allen ’s chest grows. He tries to speak, but no words come.

Behind her, Allen sees something familiar—a flicker of movement, a child’s figure crouched in the distance, her hands delicately arranging the shattered pieces of a porcelain doll. It’s Lia. His daughter. Her tiny fingers move over the doll, but the cracks are too deep, the pieces too fine.

He steps forward, but the Dreamwalker raises a hand. "Fragile," she says, her voice carrying the weight of eternity. The glass beneath his feet groans, cracking further. Lia’s hands tremble, and Allen ’s heart clenches, but he still feels nothing. No sorrow, no grief—just numbness.

He watches as Lia picks up another shard, only for it to splinter in her palm. Her lips move, but no sound reaches him.

"Help her," he tries to say, but his voice is swallowed by the growing silence.

The *Dreamwalker* tilts her head, her expression unreadable, but there’s something in her eyes—a flicker of curiosity, of knowing. "Lost," she whispers, stepping closer to Lia, her fingers brushing the air as if she could touch the girl’s fleeting figure. "Can’t fix."

Allen ’s hands clench at his sides, the urge to reach out—to *do* something—rising within him, but his limbs feel heavy, anchored to the ground. The Dreamwalker turns to him, her iridescent eyes reflecting his own face back at him, distorted and fractured. "Broken," she murmurs.

He feels it now, the weight of the word. The truth of it. His grief, once buried so deep, rises like a tide, but it’s not sorrow that comes with it—it’s emptiness. A void.

"Release," she offers, her hand outstretched. Her fingers hover just above his heart, as if she could pull the weight from him, unburdening his weight.

The Dreamwalker’s hand hovers over Allen ’s chest, her fingertips barely brushing the air between them. He feels the pressure, the pull of her offer—not physical, but something deeper. It’s like she’s reaching for the hollow place inside him, the place where his grief should be. Her touch promises release, a letting go of everything that weighs him down, everything he’s locked away.

"Release," she whispers again, her voice like a distant song, and for a moment, Allen sways toward her, drawn to the stillness she offers.

But then, from behind her, he hears it—a tiny, desperate sound. Lia.

He turns, his eyes finding her crouched beside the shattered doll. Her small hands tremble as she picks up another piece, trying so hard to fit it back together, but the pieces keep slipping through her fingers. She doesn’t cry, but there’s something in the set of her shoulders, in the way her hands shake, that twists something deep in Allen ’s chest.

He takes a step toward her, but the Dreamwalker is still in his way. She tilts her head, watching him, her eyes unreadable. She doesn’t stop him, but she doesn’t move aside either. Instead, the ground beneath his feet shifts again, the glass cracking louder, the sound echoing through the hall like a warning.

"Can’t fix," she whispers, her voice soft, but the words are sharp, like the edges of the shattered glass beneath them. "Broken."

Allen ’s jaw tightens. He feels the pull of her words, the truth in them. He is broken. Everything is. His family, his home, his heart. He can feel it all, the weight of it, pressing down on him like the very air is trying to crush him. But even as the numbness surges inside him, there’s something else, too—something fragile but insistent.

He looks at Lia again. She’s still trying, her little hands picking up each broken piece, her face set in quiet determination. She’s not giving up. She’s trying.

Allen takes another step forward, the sound of glass splintering beneath his boots, but this time he doesn’t stop. The Dreamwalker watches him with that same eerie stillness, her hand still outstretched, still offering release, but he moves past her.

"Lia," he calls, but his voice is a whisper in the vastness of the dream. She doesn’t look up, doesn’t acknowledge him, but that doesn’t stop him. He kneels beside her, his knees sinking into the shattered glass that covers the floor like snow. It should hurt, but in the dream, there’s no pain—just the sensation of everything slipping away, falling apart.

"Let me help," he whispers, reaching for one of the broken pieces.

His fingers brush the edge of the porcelain, but as soon as he touches it, the piece crumbles to dust in his hand. Lia doesn’t look at him, doesn’t speak, but she keeps trying, keeps picking up the pieces, even as they break and dissolve in her small hands.

The Dreamwalker is beside him now, her presence a cold shadow over his shoulder. "Lost," she says softly, the word wrapping around him like a shroud. "Can’t save."

Allen shakes his head, his heart pounding in his chest. "No," he whispers, though the word feels like a lie. He can’t save her. He couldn’t save any of them. But that doesn’t mean he should stop trying. He reaches for another piece of the doll, his hand steady this time, but as soon as his fingers touch it, it dissolves again, slipping through his grasp like sand.

The dream shifts. The glass beneath them cracks wider, and the walls of the hallway begin to bend and shatter. The air grows cold, and the light fades, leaving them in a world of broken glass and darkness.

"Lia," Allen whispers again, his voice breaking. He wants to hold her, to tell her it’s okay, to tell her she doesn’t have to fix the doll, that it’s not her fault, that nothing is her fault. But she’s slipping away, fading into the darkness as the dream collapses around them.

The Dreamwalker stands behind him, her eyes still gleaming in the growing dark. "Release," she whispers one last time, her voice a cold caress against his skin.

But Allen doesn’t take her hand. He watches Lia disappear into the shadows, and his heart aches with a grief too deep for words.

And then he wakes.

The memory of the dream slips away almost immediately, like smoke on the wind. Allen lies in the quiet darkness of the room, his heart pounding in his chest, but he can’t remember why. The details of the dream are already gone, leaving only a vague sense of loss, of something just out of reach.

He doesn’t know what it was, but the weight of it lingers.

He rolls over, staring up at the ceiling, his breath shallow, his chest tight. For a moment, he feels the edges of something—grief, perhaps, or maybe just the ghost of it. But like the dream, it slips away before he can grasp it, leaving him with only the hollow ache of something he can’t quite name.

In the silence of the room, Allen closes his eyes again, but sleep doesn’t come. The world outside is quiet, but inside, something is stirring—slowly, quietly, but it’s there.

**Interlude: The Gentle Anvil**  
  
The shop, bathed in a rainbow of refracting light, held the kind of stillness only found in places filled with quiet, dedicated work. As the sun filtered through the myriad of colored glass hanging in the windows, it painted the floor in hues of violet, amber, and emerald. The glow was serene, almost haunting, the light moving slowly like the ebb and flow of an unseen tide.

Tekka sat hunched over his workbench, a pair of delicate glass tongs in his thick hands as she delicately placed a molten piece of glass inside a kiln. Her movements were slow and deliberate, as always. Her dark, mahogany skin gleamed faintly in the rays of color dancing through the shop. Nearby, Jurren fumbled with a stack of glass rods, his youthful hands too eager, as always, his mouth in constant motion.

"Do you think Allen ’s cut out for this?" Jurren asked with a lopsided grin, his voice light and teasing. “I mean, he’s got the hands for it, sure, but you think he's ever going to stop brooding long enough to actually enjoy the work?”

Tekka chuckled softly, the sound deep and rumbling like distant thunder. "It takes time, lad. People carry grief in all sorts of ways. He’ll find his place. Just gotta let him be." Tekka adjusted her spectacles, watching the glass in the kiln with a practiced eye.

Jurren scratched the back of his head and shrugged. “I guess. Just feels like sometimes he’s waiting for the next thing to go wrong. It's like he’s here, but not really here, you know?”

Tekka’s dark eyes, lined with age and wisdom, flicked to her son. “The man's lost his family and his home, Jurren. You don't come back from that quickly.”

Jurren sighed, a flicker of empathy softening his usual playful tone. "Yeah, I know. Still, would it kill him to crack a smile once in a while? I tried to get him to laugh yesterday with that line about him smelling like he’d been walking for a week before standing at the forge, and he just blinked at me like I’d asked him to solve an equation."

A ghost of a smile tugged at the corner of Tekka’s mouth. "He laughed, didn’t he?"

"Well, yeah. Eventually." Jurren grinned, leaning against the counter where various tools and scraps of half-molten glass were strewn about. “But I swear, it’s like he was fighting it. I’ve seen statues more willing to crack up.”

As the two continued their easy banter, a faint shimmer of light caught Jurren's eye. He looked up just as a soft hum filled the air, growing louder as a swirl of golden light manifested in the center of the shop. The glass around them seemed to vibrate in resonance with the magic, the refracting rays bending toward the source as if pulled by an unseen force. Tekka straightened, his hands stilling over his work.

“Rost,” Tekka murmured.

The light coalesced into a floating, glowing sphere—a magical message. It flickered for a moment, then spoke in a soft, melodic tone that carried the unmistakable timbre of Rost, their distant relative.

“I am returning from Serraphine. There are objects... rare, delicate, and needing special care. I’ll arrive before nightfall. Prepare the room.”

The message faded, leaving behind only the shimmer of refracted light, the colors slowly returning to their normal dance along the walls.

Jurren raised an eyebrow. “Objects, huh? Betcha he's picked up something fancy.”
