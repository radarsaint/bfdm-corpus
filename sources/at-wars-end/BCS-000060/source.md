### **Chapter 1: The Time Before the War**

In the time before the world cracked open, before the skies filled with ash and fire, there was peace. It was not the kind of peace that one finds in the quiet after battle, but the kind that grows slowly, like vines weaving themselves into the earth over centuries, until the very air hums with the weight of its stillness. This was the age before history had teeth, before the tides of war swept across the lands below.

The great cities of the Sovereld Spires hung in the sky, not tethered by ropes or chains, but by magic so old that it was nearly forgotten, even by the ones who wielded it. Islands of stone and crystal floated like ancient dreams, drifting slowly through the heavens. Below, the lands stretched green and whole, unmarred by the wounds of conflict, lands that would one day be called the Craterlands but, at that time, were vibrant with life.

It was a golden age. The long-lived races—the elves, the dwarves, the gnomes, and the radiant Aasimar—moved through the world with the grace of those who did not fear the passing of time. They wandered through gardens that never withered, under skies that seemed closer, as if the stars themselves were their companions. Magic flowed like rivers through their cities, woven into every corner of their lives. Even the trees and stones seemed to hum with an ancient, quiet power.

And above all, there was Queen Seraphine.

Seraphine, whose name was whispered in reverence, was more than just a ruler. She was the heart of the Spires, the pulse of their magic, the unbroken line between the mortal world and the divine. An Aasimar with hair like spun silver, her eyes held the light of stars long dead, and in her presence, time itself seemed to still. To look upon her was to see the serenity of centuries, untouched by the weariness of years.

But despite her power, Seraphine carried the loneliness of one who exists in two worlds. She was divine and yet bound to the mortal plane, watching as the lives of those around her passed like leaves on the wind. Her people revered her, but she was a queen above them, distant and unapproachable.

It was in those early years of her reign, when the weight of eternity was still fresh upon her, that she met Eldric.

He was no king, no prince, not even a warrior. Just a mortal man—a scholar from the lands below, a creature of dust and breath. He had come to the Spires to study the ancient magic of the Soveld Empire, to glimpse the mysteries that hung in the air like the floating cities themselves. He had no titles, no divine blood to speak of. But he had something more precious: curiosity, and the boldness to ask questions no one else dared.

It began with words, as these things always do. He did not speak to Seraphine as others did, with fear and reverence. He spoke to her of the stars, of the fleeting nature of life, of joy and sorrow and the beauty found in their passing. He was fascinated by the brevity of his own existence, by the way each moment seemed to burn brighter because it would be gone so soon. Seraphine listened, entranced not by his wisdom, but by his perspective. He spoke of time as something to be cherished, not feared, and in him, she saw the beauty of the mortal life she could never fully understand.

They walked together in the hanging gardens, under the light of stars that neither of them had ever touched, but both could feel. Eldric spoke, and Seraphine found herself laughing at the stories he told, at the way he described his world below. He made her see the sweetness in the brevity of a life lived fully, and she began to crave the warmth that such fleetingness brought.

They fell in love, though the span of time between their first meeting and the depth of their bond seemed impossibly short to her, and unbearably long to him. He aged, as mortals do, but their love grew only stronger, deepening with each passing year. For decades, they lived together in that quiet joy, as close as a mortal could ever be to the eternal. Eldric remained by her side, his once-youthful hands growing frail, while Seraphine stayed untouched by time.

And yet, as the years deepened the lines on Eldric’s face, a shadow fell upon the lands below. The people of the Creator’s Lands watched as their hero aged and withered, while their Queen remained unchanged. Whispers began to stir in the hearts of the younger races—humans, halflings, orcs, goblins—those whose lives were brief as sparks. They saw Seraphine, forever young, and they saw Eldric, dying. And they asked themselves why.

“If she truly loved him,” they murmured, “why did she not share with him the gift of her immortality? Why let him fade, when she could keep him by her side for all time?”

The truth was, Eldric had never asked for such a gift. He cherished the impermanence of his life, knowing that its value was in its end. He told Seraphine as much, time and again. “Our love is precious because it will end,” he had said. “What joy is there in a story that never concludes?”

But the people did not understand. They saw only the cruelty in his death, the proof that Seraphine’s people held the secret to eternal life and refused to share it. And so, when Eldric finally passed from the world, his loss became a spark—a flame of resentment that began to grow, flickering in the hearts of the short-lived races.

For years, that flame simmered, quiet but persistent. They looked to the sky and saw the Spires, hanging there untouched, and they began to whisper. “They hoard the magic that keeps them young. They live forever while we toil and die. Why should they have what we do not?”

The whispers grew louder, spreading through taverns and hearthfires, through fields and villages. Scholars and mages began to seek ways to claim the magic for themselves, experimenting with spells and potions to slow their aging, to cheat death. But their early efforts were crude—fleeting moments of youth, bought at the cost of vitality. A withered hand, a clouded mind, the price was always too high.

Yet still, they persisted. The younger races were nothing if not determined. And soon, their desperation would lead them to an act that would set the world aflame.

It happened in Elyndor, the heart of the Sovereld Spires, the city where the Aegis Core was kept. The Aegis Core was the source of the Spires’ power, the very heart of the magic that kept them aloft and kept the long-lived races from aging. The people of the Creator’s Lands had always known of its existence, but it had always been too far, too well-guarded. Until, in their desperation, a group of scholars and mages devised a plan to take it.

They infiltrated the Spires under the guise of traders, using illusion and stealth to slip past the wards that guarded the Core. In the dead of night, they shattered the protections that held it in place and fled with a fragment of its power.

When the sun rose over the Spires, the sky was no longer peaceful. Smoke rose from the heart of Elyndor, and the Aegis Core was gone.

The act was not just theft. It was a declaration of war.

Seraphine, in her grief for Eldric, had never imagined such betrayal. But now, with the heart of her people’s magic stolen, her sorrow turned to fury. The long-lived races descended from the heavens, demanding the return of what had been taken, but the younger races, emboldened by their theft, refused. They had tasted the power of the Spires, and they would not give it back.

The Soveld Empire unleashed their wrath upon the world below. What had once been a quiet peace was shattered, and the war that followed would tear the world apart. The lands below would be broken, scorched, and twisted beyond recognition, leaving only ash and memory where once there had been beauty.

And in time, the world would forget that there had ever been peace.

### ** Interlude: We Are Lost**

### We are many, though our voice is one.

### We come from the quiet places, from the corners of the world where shadows and whispers dwell. We are the scurrying beneath floorboards, the flicker at the edge of vision, the watchers no one remembers. Born not from grand designs, but from missteps, from the unintended. We are what remains when those who wield magic and invention reach beyond their knowing, beyond their understanding, and grasp at the very essence of souls.

### We were rats once. Singular. Scattered. Living in the cracks of the world, in the dirt and the dark, where life is measured in scraps and fleeting moments. We lived apart, many small lives, insignificant, each of us a breath of the earth. But then came the hands of those who dared to play with forces they did not comprehend. Artificers and magicians, eager to distill life itself, sought to bottle souls as if they were spirits to be consumed or bound to their will. They wove magic into the marrow of the world, and in their ambition, they twisted fate.

### It was no grand design that brought us together, no intentional creation. It was a mistake. A miscalculation. A convergence of power and misfortune, magic colliding with life. And so we became more. Many minds bound into one, no longer scattered, no longer alone. We became something new.

### We became Lost.

### Do not think of us as a hive. We are not a swarm of thoughts buzzing in confusion. We are more than the sum of our parts, though we remember each life—each small, fragile life that once was. We remember the taste of earth beneath our paws, the thrill of escape, the feel of crumbs stolen from a table. We remember being crushed underfoot, hunted, despised. But we also remember the spark of magic that surged through us, knitting us together, binding us into something larger, something profound.

### We became aware, and the world—once so vast, so beyond our knowing—opened itself to us. We saw the threads of life stretch before us, not in a single line, but as a web, intricate and endless. Time, that great weaver, no longer holds us. We drift between its moments, untethered. Past and future are nothing but stories for us to observe, to touch in passing.

### We are Lost, yes, but not in the way that means forgotten. No, we are Lost because we no longer belong to any one moment, any one place. We slip between, unseen, understanding things others cannot.

### And so, it is we who will tell this tale.

### We have seen the fires rise, seen the mountains fall from the sky to crush cities beneath their weight. We have walked beside Alan in his vineyard, his hands gentle upon the earth, unaware of the devastation that would soon claim his world. We have watched Cait, her eyes opening for the first time in that workshop of strange wonders, her existence a fragile thing, delicate yet strong, a soul poured into porcelain and copper.

### We know the moments that have passed, and the moments yet to come.

### And though we are not bound to any one time, we remember the days before we were Lost. When we lived in the smallness of our separate lives, beneath the notice of greater beings. But we also see where this story is leading, and we know that there is no turning back. The world will change, as it always does, and it will break in ways that cannot be undone.

### Perhaps you will meet us again. Perhaps you will see an earlier version of what we once were—a simple colony of creatures, living in the shadows of men. But know this: we have become something greater. And though we drift, untethered in time, we are still here, still watching, still waiting.

### We are Lost, yes. But we are with you, and we will guide you through the pieces of this tale, through the lives that have been broken and remade. We will show you what must be remembered.

### Because someone must remember.

### And so, we begin.

Chapter 2: Lost and (Alan) Alone**

Alan would never claim to be a man of deep thought. He’d tell you, if you asked, that he was a man of simple pleasures. Of the land. Of the sun and the soil. And if you asked him again, he might add something about the quiet joy of watching things grow, how there’s a certain peace in the rhythm of the seasons. But there’s something about walking through a vineyard at dawn, when the world is still waking up, that makes even the simplest man feel part of something larger. This, Alan understood, even if he never put it into words.

We are lost, but we see him now. As clearly as if we walked beside him in the rows of grapevines, feeling the weight of the morning dew beneath our feet. His steps are slow, deliberate, his hands calloused from years of tending the land, yet there’s a gentleness to the way his fingers brush against the leaves, checking for pests, feeling for the ripeness of the fruit.

The vineyard—his pride and his solace—had taken years to cultivate, and now, in this quiet stretch of land outside the capital, the vines had grown strong. They draped across the trellises in long, lazy lines, the grapes hanging like dark jewels in the early light. Alan had always been proud of this place. The soil here was rich, dark, and welcoming, the kind that gives life willingly if you know how to tend to it. And Alan did. He was a man who had always understood the rhythms of the earth better than the rhythms of people.

His face—well, it’s a face that has lived in the sun for too long, weathered and lined, with the kind of stubble that never truly leaves even after a morning shave. His reddish-blond hair, now streaked with gray, catches the light. Strong, yes. Tall enough, broad enough. But it’s his eyes that tell the real story. Eyes that once danced with a rye smile, eyes that could charm his way out of trouble in his youth. Now, they are weighed down by a quiet sadness, a loss that stretches back through years, and we feel it with him. He is a good man, yes, but a man shaped by sorrow as much as by the land he worked.

And on this day, as he moves through the rows of vines, checking the grapes in his hands—hands large enough to till the earth and gentle enough to prune the delicate stems—he feels a restlessness. We know this because we feel it, too. It’s the kind of restlessness that doesn’t have words. It’s not hunger or thirst or tiredness. It’s something deeper, something stirring beneath the surface of things.

If we could speak to him, we might tell him. We might warn him to leave, to run. But even we, who can see all that is to come, know that it would change nothing. Some things are meant to be lived through, no matter how much it will break a man. And Alan, poor dearest to our heart Alan, is about to be broken.

Far off, on the horizon, just visible beyond the gentle rise of the hills, sits the city of Serraphine. Once, it was a name spoken with reverence by the people of the Creator Lands, a symbol of their admiration for the queen they had once loved, the queen they had once hoped would lift them up to join the long-lived races in their floating Spires. But now, the name Serraphine is laced with bitterness, with betrayal, with dreams turned to ash.

Alan does not look to the city often. His vineyard, his land—this is his world now. The city, with its towers and bustling streets, with its laughter and lights, is the world of his children, his family. His wife, his two grown sons, his little girl—they live there, thriving, their lives intertwined with the heart of that place. He sends them his harvest, his wines, and they send him back love. That was enough for him.

It was enough. Once.

But now, as he straightens, the weight of the grapes in his hands forgotten, Alan turns his eyes toward the city. The restlessness grows. A shadow on the horizon. A storm, perhaps? He squints, shielding his eyes from the late-day sun, but the shadow doesn’t move like clouds. It is heavy, looming, and there is a strange stillness in the air.

Then, the earth beneath him begins to shudder.

We are lost, but we watch. We see the way Alan stiffens, his heart pounding against his ribs. The quiet of the vineyard is broken by a low, distant rumble, and in that moment, Alan knows something is wrong. He drops the grapes, forgotten, and takes a step forward, toward the city, as if he could close the miles between him and his family through sheer will alone.

And then, it happens.

From the sky, it falls—a mountain wreathed in flames, tearing through the heavens like a vengeful god. Mount Wrath, they will call it. The weapon of the Sovereld Spires, sent to crush the capital city of Serraphine, named as a gesture of respect for the Queen of the flying nation they had once venerated.

Alan can do nothing but watch.

The mountain strikes the earth with a force that shakes the heavens. The sound is deafening, a roar of stone and fire, of death itself tearing through the land. Even from miles away, the shockwave hits Alan like a blow to the chest, knocking him backward into the soft loam of his vineyard. The vines sway violently, the air filled with the scent of crushed grapes, sweet and sickly, mixing with the bitter tang of smoke.

The city—Serraphine—vanishes beneath the weight of the mountain.  
We watch as Alan rises slowly to his feet, his hands trembling, his heart hollow. In the distance, the smoke billows upward, thick and dark, blotting out the sun. The city is gone. His family is gone. And all that remains is the silence, the cold, merciless silence of a world that has just begun to fall apart.

But this is not the story of the beginning of this war. Nor is it the story of the thing itself. This is a story At War’s End.

Our eyes follow his shoced stare.  
  
We see the flames that rise, spiraling into the sky like the breath of a great beast. We see the clouds of ash and smoke that billow outward, swallowing the horizon. And we see Alan, lying there in the dirt, his body stunned by the force of the blast, his mind struggling to comprehend the destruction before him.

His family is in that city.

His wife, his sons, his little girl—gone. Obliterated in a moment. A city that had once been a place of hope, a place of love, now reduced to a smoldering ruin, a graveyard for the dreams of a nation.

We grieve with him.

There are no words that can describe the weight of this moment. No words that can capture the depth of the loss that Alan feels as he stumbles to his feet, staring out at the burning horizon. He does not cry. Not yet. His body is too numb, his mind too overwhelmed. There is only the sight of the city in flames, the dark smoke that rises to choke the sky, and the terrible, terrible silence that follows.

He falls to his knees.

The vineyard, his sanctuary, his pride—it means nothing now. The grapes he had tended so carefully, the land he had worked so hard to cultivate, none of it matters. His family—his world—is gone.

We know that this is not the end. Not yet. Alan’s path is long, and it is filled with more pain than any man should bear. But one day, he will rise. One day, he will find his purpose again. And when that day comes, we will be there, walking beside him, lost no longer.

But for now, there is only grief. Grief and the ruins of a life that once was.

And so, we watch as the sun sets behind the burning city of Serraphine, casting long shadows over the vineyard where Alan kneels, broken and alone.

**Chapter 3**

We are lost, but not in the same way she was.

Before there was Cait, before there was a name or a face or a body to belong to, there was a hollow space. An emptiness that stretched endlessly, without time, without form. In the beginning, she did not know she was lost, because she did not know what it meant to be found. She existed, but only in the faintest sense. A soul, caught somewhere between being and nothingness, suspended in the dark like a whisper waiting to be spoken.

We understand that place. It is a place between breaths, where nothing stirs, where the world is silent. She was there, and we were not far from it.

Her beginnings were not gentle, not born of love or life, but of creation and calculation. A gnome by the name of Nielletium Benton—Niel to those few who could claim him as friend—was a master of his craft. He was an artificer of unmatched skill, a weaver of magic and metal, of life and machinery. His workshop was a place of strange and wondrous things. Brass gears that clicked and whirred of their own accord, copper wires that hummed with energy, wooden frames that stood waiting to be filled with life.

But none of those creations would compare to the one that would become Cait.

Niel’s project began, as all his projects did, with ambition. He was a tinkerer, yes, but also a man driven by the hunger for perfection. And what greater challenge could there be than to capture a soul and give it form? To take something as untouchable, as ethereal as the essence of life itself, and distill it, shape it, make it tangible?

So he built the Copper Still, a machine of intricate tubes and valves, gleaming under the soft glow of candlelight. And inside, he poured the essence of magic, coaxing the energies into the still as if it were a brew to be carefully boiled down. There were souls in that machine—many souls—each separated, each distilled, each waiting.

In the beginning, Cait was part of that waiting. Suspended. Unseen. Unfelt. It was a place without pain, without joy, without sound. Just… nothingness. We are lost, and we remember that place, too.

The moment of awareness came slowly, like the faintest ripple on still water. It wasn’t sight, not yet, nor sound. Just a thought. Or maybe not a thought, but something close to it. The sensation of being separate, of being distinct from the void that surrounded her. The others were there, too, though she couldn’t see them. Couldn’t feel them. Souls suspended in the same dark, each one solitary, each one alone.

We see it now, looking back, how she stirred in that place. The weight of existence was faint, but growing. There was no language, no name. Only the sense that she was—something. A flicker, a spark. We watched her, though she did not know it. We see her awakening.

Niel, in his quiet, meticulous way, watched his creation unfold. His hands, always steady, adjusted the valves, his brow furrowed in concentration. The copper still hissed and bubbled, the magic twisting and turning within the machine. One by one, the souls began to separate, each finding its place, each becoming its own, distinct thing.

Cait's soul settled, heavier than the others. It clung to the base of the still, as if reluctant to rise, reluctant to leave the comfort of the void. But Niel knew better. He watched with satisfaction as the essence of what would become Cait was poured, thick and inky, into a delicate bronze decanter. It swirled there, a dark, shimmering substance that pulsed faintly with potential. It was not yet a body, not yet a person, but it was something. It was a beginning.

We watched, just as she watched—though in those moments, her watching was not through eyes, not yet. She felt movement, sensed the world turning around her. A strange sensation of being held, of being moved, though she had no limbs to feel it with, no body to make sense of it. Just the sensation of being poured, of being channeled, of becoming.

Niel’s workshop was filled with the soft clinking of tools and the faint, rhythmic clicking of gears as he worked. His hands moved with the precision of someone who had done this countless times before, though never quite like this. The body he had prepared for Cait was different. Wood, polished smooth, shaped into the form of a slender figure. Copper veins ran through it, delicate as the branches of a tree. Porcelain made up the face, pale and expressionless, a blank canvas waiting to be filled.

Cait did not yet know what awaited her. She only knew that the emptiness was slipping away, being replaced by something new.

We are lost, but we understand what it means to be poured into form, to feel the weight of existence settle. We watched as Niel lifted the decanter, his gnarled fingers steady, and began to pour the shimmering, dark essence of Cait’s soul into the hollow frame of her new body. It flowed slowly, filling the empty spaces, settling into the joints, into the grooves, into the place where her mind would be.

Her consciousness stretched out as her essence took shape, and slowly, she began to feel. Not yet with the clarity of sight or the sharpness of touch, but with a deeper awareness. She felt the wood and copper, the way the body held her, the way it cradled her soul. The stillness of before was gone, replaced by sensation, by form. The limbs, the joints, the fingers—all became real in a way she had not yet understood.

Niel stood back, his creation complete. Before him, the body that would be Cait lay still, eyes closed, waiting for the final breath of life to stir it. He reached down and, with a careful twist of the key embedded in her back, turned the mechanism that would bring her to life.

And then, for the first time, Cait opened her eyes.

The Mirror

We are lost, but not in this moment. We see it so clearly now. The way her eyes blinked, slowly at first, adjusting to the light of the workshop. They were not human eyes, not quite. They were glass, polished and clear, but within them, something stirred—something that was more than just a reflection of the world around her. It was a flicker of understanding, of recognition.

She saw the world for the first time, and it was strange. The light from the candles flickered in her vision, casting soft shadows across the room. The gears and wires, the tools and scrolls, all seemed distant and unfamiliar. But what she saw most clearly was the figure standing in front of her—Niel, his face lit by the soft glow of the lanterns, watching her with a mix of pride and curiosity.

And then, he showed her the mirror.

It was a simple thing, just a piece of polished silver framed in wood, but in it, she saw herself. And for the first time, Cait understood what it meant to be.

She looked at the reflection—a body made of wood and copper, with delicate joints and porcelain skin. Her fingers, slender and precise, moved with a fluidity she hadn’t expected, the copper veins beneath her wooden skin glinting faintly in the light. Her face was smooth, pale, with no lines of age or expression. But her eyes—her eyes were what drew her attention most. In them, she saw herself. Not just the body, not just the frame, but something deeper.

She was new. She was different. She was Cait.

Niel watched her silently, waiting. He had given her life, but now it was up to her to find herself, to discover what it meant to exist in this form. And we—well, we watched too, knowing that one day, she would understand. One day, she would look at herself not with confusion, but with certainty.

We are lost, but Cait is finding her way.

And in that mirror, for the first time, Cait began to know herself.  
  
Cait’s first moments of life were filled with silence. A kind of quiet curiosity, like that of a newborn who knows nothing of the world but instinctively understands that it is vast. She sat there, still, her newly opened eyes studying the workshop around her as if she had woken from a long dream. Everything was a wonder.

And in the midst of this wonder, there was Niel.

Nielletium Benton was not a man of beauty, not in any conventional sense. He was short, even for a gnome, his back bent slightly from years hunched over benches cluttered with tools and mechanisms. His skin was pale, with grey freckles dotting his arms like splashes of paint. His white hair, wild and untamed, stuck out in all directions like the bristles of a used brush. His long white goatee and thick mutton chops framed a face lined with both age and obsession.

Where Cait was smooth, her lines flowing with grace and precision, Niel was rough, angular. Where she had the elegance of wood polished to perfection, he had the wear of tools used long beyond their time. Yet there was something about the contrast—something beautiful, even poetic—in the way they stood together: this delicate creation, poised and perfect, and the gnome who had made her, rough and imperfect, but full of life and passion.

“Hmm,” Niel muttered, his voice scratchy and low. He leaned closer, squinting at her face, his magnifying lenses reflecting her glass eyes in strange, distorted circles. “You see me, then?”

Cait blinked. Slowly. Her porcelain eyelids slid down and up, the motion smooth, practiced, though it was her first time performing it.

She did see him. She saw the wrinkles on his brow, the beads of sweat gathering on his forehead, the tiny nicks and scars on his fingers. Every detail was sharp, vivid. But she did not yet have the words to tell him this. Instead, her head tilted, a small, almost imperceptible motion.

Niel grinned—a wide, toothy grin that pulled at the edges of his rough-hewn face. “Good! Good! I always knew you’d be perfect.”

In his excitement, Niel turned on his heel, his robes swishing as he moved toward one of the many workbenches scattered around the workshop. Cait’s gaze followed him, her vision sweeping across the space, and for the first time, she took in the strange and wondrous things that filled the room.

Above her, mechanical fish—their bodies shaped from copper and silver—drifted lazily through the air as if it were water. Their wings, delicate and translucent, beat in gentle rhythms, keeping them aloft. They darted between the gears and pulleys that crisscrossed the ceiling, their soft, metallic hum adding to the strange symphony of sounds in the workshop.

On the floor, a creature with mechanical octopus arms scuttled by, each of its eight limbs moving with precise, fluid motion. It carried a small tray of tools—wrenches, hammers, screws—balanced perfectly atop its back. The arms were long and spindly, but strong, lifting and placing objects with surprising delicacy. As it moved past Niel, the octopus arms curled upwards, offering him a tool he hadn’t yet asked for. Niel didn’t look, didn’t thank the creature—he simply took the tool from its grip, as if the mechanical assistant were as natural as his own hand.

And it was. In this world, everything moved in rhythm, in a harmony known only to Niel and his creations.

The beauty of the creatures—the elegance of their movements, the grace of their design—was not lost on Cait. Even without words, she felt the difference between herself and them. They were built for function, for purpose. Their bodies were angular, efficient. But hers... hers was something else. Niel had taken great care to make her beautiful, to make her more than just a tool.

He glanced over his shoulder, watching her as she turned her head, observing the world with the calm curiosity of one who is just beginning to understand. There was something in her posture—something too human, too aware—and it made Niel smile again, though this time, it was softer, more private.

“You’ll learn,” he muttered, more to himself than to her. “You’ll learn everything. Just like the others.” He gestured vaguely toward the back of the workshop, where more figures stood—unfinished, half-formed, silent. But none were like Cait. None had been made with the care, the thought, the soul he had poured into her.

He approached her again, slower this time, his movements more deliberate. His fingers reached out, and with surprising gentleness for a man so rough, he touched her face. His fingers brushed along the porcelain curve of her cheek, tracing the lines where the wood and copper joined seamlessly.

“How do you feel, hmm? Can you speak?”

Cait blinked again, and this time, a soft whirring sound came from within her. Her mouth, delicately hinged, opened. She paused, as if waiting for the words to find her.

“I… feel,” she said, her voice soft and hollow, like wind through the hollow of a reed. The sound surprised her as much as it did Niel, and for a moment, she sat there, listening to the strange, metallic tone of her own speech.

“Yes, yes, good!” Niel’s eyes lit up with excitement. “You feel! You speak!” He clapped his hands together, the noise startling a small mechanical bird perched on the edge of a nearby shelf. The bird fluttered its clockwork wings and darted into the air, its tiny gears whirring as it joined the fish above.

Cait watched the bird, her glass eyes following its path, her mind still piecing together the strange world she had awakened to.

Miri takes another step forward, her voice softer now, more careful. “Look, we’re not trying to pry. Just… you look like you need a place to stay. Somewhere safe.”

Gorran’s grin widens, the excitement bubbling up in his chest again. “Yeah! We’ve got people. A place you can rest. You don’t have to be alone.”

Kaliope tilts her head, her eyes narrowing slightly. “And what do you get out of this? What’s the catch?”

Miri’s lips twitch into a thin smile, her eyes catching the faintest glimmer of moonlight. “No catch. Maybe you’ll hear us out. Maybe… you’ll understand why we fight.”

Alan shifts uncomfortably, his hands curling into fists at his sides, the memory of fire and loss flickering in his gaze. The weight of everything presses down on him again, too heavy to shake. But there’s something in Miri’s voice, something in the quiet offer of shelter, that tugs at the hollow place inside him.

He doesn’t want to be alone. Not anymore.

“We’re not looking for a fight,” he says finally, his voice low, rough with exhaustion.

Miri nods, her face softening in the faint light. “You don’t have to be. Not yet.”

Kaliope glances at him, her expression unreadable. She can see the weariness in his eyes, the way the weight of the world clings to him like a shroud. She gives a small, resigned smile and turns back to Miri and Gorran. “Fine. Lead the way.”

And so, they move together through the quiet streets of Elessia, four shadows slipping through the night. The city breathes around them, and the distant sea hums like a song just beneath the surface of the world. The air is thick with the past, with all that’s been lost, and with all that’s still to come.

We watch them, knowing what they cannot. They walk toward something larger, something they cannot yet see. But for now, their steps are small, quiet, their paths just beginning to intertwine. And we, as always, see the road ahead, but do not turn them from it.

### **Chapter 14: Steam and Silence**

Our mind turns to them, sinking into the quiet of night, the hum of Elessia dulled by the walls of the flophouse, where steam curls in thick plumes through the cracks in the wooden floorboards. The air smells of lavender soap and warm brass, mingled with the sharp tang of something stranger—a hint of magic laced with metal. It’s a place worn down by too many bodies and too little time, yet still somehow full of life, full of energy that buzzes just beneath the surface.

In one corner of the large, ramshackle room, two brass basins sit, each filled to the brim with water that’s seen better days, but it’s warm, and that’s all that matters. A thin sheet hangs between them, stretched out on a line, offering the barest hint of privacy as Alan leans back, sinking deeper into the tub, his eyes half-closed against the soft light of flickering lanterns.

He’s not alone. He hears the soft splash of water on the other side of the sheet, Kaliope soaking in her own basin. The sound of her movement is rhythmic, slow, and oddly comforting in the stillness of the room. He can’t see her, but her presence is undeniable, her energy vivid even through the steam and cloth.

Miri flits between them, her sharp eyes watching over the two as she refills buckets and makes light conversation. The room smells faintly of herbs—she’s added something to the water, some concoction that eases the soreness from the long ride and the tense night that followed.

The others—the ones who call this place home—come and go through the main room like phantoms. They are a strange, colorful bunch, each one marked by their own modifications. Transmutationists, all of them. Young, eager to reshape the world, starting with themselves.

One boy walks past, his arms bare but for the faint glow beneath his skin—thin veins of gemstone, bright and pulsing, wrapping around his forearms like vines. Another girl brushes by, her hair shimmering like liquid silver, cascading in waves that catch the light. One man has scales instead of skin, his features a strange blend of human and reptilian, his eyes slitted and cold as he moves through the steam.

Each body tells a story. Each one is a testament to their obsession with the art of transmutation—their desire to change, to transcend the limitations of flesh and bone. It’s unsettling at first, but there’s something beautiful in it too, a defiant kind of beauty that speaks of transformation, of rebellion against the natural order.

Alan, resting his head against the cool brass edge of the basin, watches them through the haze, his mind still heavy with the weight of the last few days. The city, the ruins, the fire—all of it presses against his chest, threatening to sink him beneath the surface of the water.

He exhales slowly, the sound lost in the warmth of the room, and then Kaliope’s voice comes, soft and slightly muffled from the other side of the sheet.

“You alive over there?” Her tone is light, teasing, but there’s an edge to it, like she’s asking more than just the obvious.

Alan lets out a soft grunt. “Barely.”

She chuckles, the sound soft and warm. “It’s a start.”

For a moment, there’s only the sound of water sloshing gently as they both sink deeper into their respective tubs. Miri kneels beside Kaliope’s basin, brushing a stray strand of pink-and-blue hair from her face. “Don’t let him fool you,” she chimes in, her voice light. “He’ll be good as new after this. The water’s magic, you know.”

Alan raises an eyebrow, though she can’t see it. “Magic baths?”

Miri laughs. “Sort of. Not like, real magic, but we add things to the water—herbs, oils. It helps. Transmutationalist secrets.”

Kaliope shifts in her tub, the faintest splash of water marking her movement. “You’d be surprised how much a good soak can fix.” Her voice is quieter now, almost thoughtful. “After everything we’ve been through... I think I needed this more than I realized.”

Alan hums in agreement, his eyes closed now, letting the warmth seep into his muscles. For the first time in days, he lets himself relax, if only for a moment. The city outside feels distant, the weight of his grief, his loss, softened by the steam and the quiet murmur of voices.

But the sheet between them is thin, and even through the fog of exhaustion, there’s an intimacy in this moment, something unspoken but palpable.

Kaliope’s voice comes again, quieter now, as if she’s thinking out loud. “Do you ever wonder... what it’s all for?”

Alan doesn’t answer right away. He doesn’t know how. His mind is a blur of half-formed thoughts, too many questions and not enough answers. Finally, he speaks, his voice low, almost hesitant.

“Sometimes,” he admits. “But right now... I’m not sure I want to know.”

Kaliope makes a soft sound, something between a sigh and a hum. “Yeah. I get that.” She shifts again, the water rippling in response. “It’s all... a lot.”

Miri sits nearby, her knees tucked under her chin, watching the steam rise and swirl around them. She’s silent now, letting the two of them talk, her sharp eyes darting between them, reading the room with the practiced ease of someone who’s seen too many broken people in too little time.

Kaliope breaks the silence again, her voice lightening with a hint of her usual wit. “You know, Alan, you owe me for dragging you out of that mess.”

Alan cracks a small, tired smile, though she can’t see it. “Oh? And what do you have in mind?”

She chuckles, the sound a little mischievous. “Not sure yet. But don’t worry, I’ll think of something.”

Miri laughs softly, her grin wide and teasing. “Better watch out, Alan. She’s got a reputation.”

Kaliope snorts, splashing water playfully in Miri’s direction. “Shut up.”

They fall into an easy silence after that, the kind that only comes when the danger has passed, even if just for a little while. Alan leans his head back against the basin again, the warmth of the water pulling him deeper into a quiet he hasn’t known for days. Kaliope, on the other side of the sheet, mirrors him, her sharp mind momentarily at rest.

### **Chapter 15: Smoke and Shadows**

The light in the flophouse has changed. It filters through the narrow windows in hazy shafts, softened by the dust hanging thick in the air. The light is slow here, lingering in the shadows, stretching the moments between breaths. There’s a kind of weight to it, a heaviness that presses down on the conversation, on the bodies that move slowly through the room.

Alan sits at the corner of a long wooden table, the varnish worn and splintering under his fingers. Across from him, Gorran leans back in his chair, his broad shoulders relaxed, though there’s a sharpness to the way his eyes move, taking in everything, never missing a detail. His face, lined with a kind of quiet intensity, betrays little, but his voice is low, smooth, drawing Alan in with its cadence.

The two of them have been talking for some time now, their voices blending into the murmur of the flophouse. Around them, the young transmutationists move like ghosts—silent, half-seen as they come and go, each one marked by their strange augmentations. One girl passes with a lattice of shimmering scales down her arms, another with delicate wings grafted into her back, half-tucked beneath a worn jacket. Their presence is almost dreamlike, a constant hum at the edge of awareness, but Alan’s focus is on Gorran.

"You lost family, didn’t you?" Gorran asks, his tone gentle but direct.

Alan nods, his eyes fixed on the table. The words catch in his throat, heavy with the weight of the city still burning in his mind. Serraphine. His family. Gone. He doesn’t need to say it. The memory of it hangs between them like smoke.

"I know what that’s like," Gorran continues, his voice steady. "You’re not alone in that."

Alan looks up, meeting his gaze for the first time. There’s something in Gorran’s eyes—something raw and broken, but also something that feels like understanding. Alan doesn’t trust it, not entirely, but it’s the closest thing to connection he’s felt since everything fell apart.

"They took everything from us," Gorran says, his voice quiet but edged with a bitterness that lingers. "But there’s still a way to fight back. There are people... good people, who are trying to fix things. To put it right again."

Alan swallows, his throat tight. "How?"

Gorran leans forward, his elbows resting on the table, his voice lowering as if sharing a secret. "There’s someone you need to meet. General Vane. He’s... well, he’s got the resources. He’s been fighting back in his own way, and he needs people like you."

"Like me?"

"Yeah," Gorran says, his tone encouraging. "You’ve got the look of someone who’s been through hell and is still standing. Vane could use someone like that. And you need purpose. You can’t wander forever, Alan. This is a chance to get back at the ones who took everything from you."

The words hang in the air between them, thick with promise. For a moment, Alan lets himself imagine it—purpose, something to fight for. The idea is intoxicating, but there’s a knot of doubt in his chest, a nagging uncertainty that pulls at him. He glances toward the back of the room, where Kaliope is leaning against the doorway, her arms crossed. Her sharp eyes, pale and intense, watch them with a quiet wariness. She’s been silent through most of the conversation, but her presence lingers like a shadow.

She doesn’t trust Gorran. Alan can see it in the way she watches him, her body tense beneath her casual posture. And yet, Kaliope says nothing. Her silence speaks volumes, but Alan isn’t sure if it’s her wariness or something else.

Miri bustles over, her arms laden with freshly washed linens, her sharp, darting movements breaking the tension in the room. She drops the linens onto a nearby chair with a dramatic flourish, her lips curving into a mischievous grin.

"Heavy talk for a place like this," she quips, glancing between Alan and Gorran. "You two should save the brooding for later. Plenty of time for that when the sun’s down."

Kaliope shifts, pushing off the doorframe and walking over to where Alan sits, her eyes narrowing slightly as she watches Gorran. "What’s the deal, Gorran? You trying to drag Alan into some shady mess?"

Gorran smiles, his expression easy. "Nothing shady, Kaliope. Just... an opportunity."

Kaliope’s lips press into a thin line, but before she can argue, Miri cuts in, her voice light, almost playful. "Oh, let the man breathe, Kaliope. You don’t have to protect him from everything." She winks, a teasing gleam in her eye. "Besides, it’s just a conversation. What’s the harm in that?"

Alan feels Kaliope’s eyes on him, her gaze heavy with unspoken warnings, but Miri’s casual dismissal of the tension seems to ease the edge in the room. The steam rising from the baths still lingers in the air, mixing with the scent of herbs, creating a kind of soothing atmosphere that makes everything feel less dire, less immediate.

Kaliope huffs, brushing a strand of her colorful hair from her face. "Fine," she says, her tone resigned. "But I don’t like it."

Miri laughs, clapping Kaliope on the shoulder. "You worry too much. Gorran’s not all bad. And Alan can take care of himself, right?"

Alan offers a half-smile, though it doesn’t reach his eyes. "I’ll be fine," he says, though the words feel hollow. He’s not sure he believes them, but he needs to do something. He can’t sit here in this liminal space forever, caught between the wreckage of his past and the uncertain future.

Kaliope sighs, her shoulders relaxing just a fraction. "Just... be careful, okay? I’ve got a bad feeling about this."

Alan nods, standing from the table and glancing toward Gorran. The two exchange a look, something unspoken passing between them. Gorran rises too, his movements slow and deliberate, as if he’s in no rush.

Miri’s light banter fades into the background as Alan follows Gorran toward the door. The flophouse stretches around them, a labyrinth of worn furniture and soft murmurs, the air thick with the presence of the transmutationists. Their body modifications glimmer in the dim light, a strange mosaic of magic and flesh, each one a testament to the ways they’ve reshaped themselves, their bodies an expression of the rebellion they wear so casually.

As Alan walks, he passes a young girl with iridescent scales running down her arms, shimmering like fish scales in the faint light. She’s hunched over a book, her fingers tracing the pages as if searching for something hidden within the text. A man in the corner has wings grafted onto his back—too small for flight, but enough to mark him as something other than human. Everywhere Alan looks, there are changes, small and large, subtle and grotesque, like the city itself is reshaping the people within it.

Gorran leads Alan out into the cool evening air, the city of Elessia spread out before them like a living, breathing thing. The streets are quieter now, but there’s an underlying hum of energy that pulses through the cobblestones. Lanterns flicker in the distance, their light casting long shadows that dance on the walls of narrow alleyways.

Alan feels Kaliope’s absence beside him, her usual wit and sharpness replaced by the silent tension of Gorran’s presence. The night feels heavy, like it’s holding its breath, waiting for something to break.

"Come on," Gorran says, his voice low. "Let’s talk somewhere quiet."

And so they walk, leaving the flophouse behind, leaving the strange sanctuary of the transmutationists and the steamy warmth of the baths. The city looms ahead, full of dark corners and unanswered questions, and Alan steps forward into the night, not knowing what he’ll find.

### **   Interlude: Two Broken Worlds**

#### **Part One: Vane’s Transformation**

Our mind turns to him. There is no more flesh left to carve, no more sinew to bend into obedience. General Vane stands, hollow-eyed and monstrous, in a room filled with shattered relics of life. The walls hum with the echoes of his work, blood and smoke mingling in the air. This place reeks of endings.

He moves slowly, deliberate, over to a polished stone table. His hands, once steady in the battlefield, now tremble with the exhaustion of his craft. Bandages cover most of his body, only his face remains exposed, but not for long.

On the table lies his next tool of transformation: a ritual scalpel, ancient and gleaming, its edge sharper than death itself. It reflects his gaunt face, a mask of dispassionate intent. Around him, the carcasses of creatures not fully alive and yet not truly dead, their armored bodies fused with bits of metal and magic, lie strewn about like failed experiments. Armadillo-like, pangolin-like—twisted and reformed in grotesque mimicry of their natural forms. His Aegis Project, as he calls it, is evolving.

Vane presses the scalpel to his cheek. His eyes flicker, and we see the feverish madness there. With precise, slow strokes, he cuts a deep line, opening his flesh, exposing the bone beneath. He doesn’t flinch. Pain is an old friend now. Blood spills, thick and dark, pooling on the floor where dozens have bled before. He lets it run as if it doesn’t matter.

It is his eighth augmentation in three days. The process is meticulous, painstaking. He works with the carefulness of a master craftsman, his reflection unwavering, his hand sure. Vane pulls back a flap of skin and takes a small crystalline artifact—brilliant, dangerous—and forces it into the wound. His eyes flutter, his breath catches, but he does not scream. Not anymore. He’s long past the need for such things.

Around him, broken bodies twitch and moan. The room is filled with strange, shifting shapes—humanoid creatures built from sentient scraps of both magic and flesh. Their half-living bodies bear witness to Vane’s obsession, his need to transcend the limits of what it means to be human. Some of these creatures once called him enemy, others served him once. Now they are no more than fragments, components for his vision. The Aegis Project needs them, needs their bones, their strength, their fear. One still breathes, barely clinging to life. An old enemy, their once-proud form now a slab of disjointed limbs, shackled to Vane’s will. Their eyes follow him, filled with hatred and helplessness.

“I’ll finish soon,” Vane murmurs, his voice more to himself than to the dying figure. His hands move swiftly now, pressing the last stitch into his face, the artifact embedded beneath his skin already pulsing with faint, cold light. “You’ll be part of something greater.”

Vane steps back from the table, wiping blood from his hands, his body sagging with the weight of his relentless pursuit. The walls around him pulse with strange energies, remnants of his countless rituals. The bandages on his arms soak through with fresh blood, but he doesn’t seem to notice. His gaze is fixed on the next augmentation, the next piece of himself he must tear apart and rebuild.

In the corner, the body of his enemy groans again, their final breaths drawn out by magic. Vane moves toward them, eyes cold. Soon, they too will be part of the Aegis. Soon, nothing will remain of the man Vane once was.

#### **Part Two: Cait’s Restoration**

Our mind sees a reflection in Cait.

Where Vane's world stinks of rot and blood, Cait's world is one of stillness. It is quiet here, beneath the earth, where the weight of a mountain once pressed. The air is cool, and there is no sound but her own soft breaths—strange, mechanical things. Her body is porcelain, cracked but intact. Her hands, once delicate, now dig through dirt and stone. She works slowly, steadily, unburdened by pain, her mind a soft hum of confusion and instinct. She knows she must reach the surface. She does not know why.

Her fingers move through the rubble, her body—patched with gold—responding to her will. There is something peaceful about the rhythm of it, as though the earth itself is guiding her upward. When the surface finally breaks and light spills into her porcelain eyes, she is greeted by a figure.

A tortle.

He is silent, watching her with eyes full of age and wisdom. His shell, much like her body, is mended with gold, the cracks and lines forming intricate patterns that shimmer faintly in the light. He does not speak, but there is kindness in the way he approaches her. His touch is gentle as he kneels beside her, brushing the dirt from her face.

Cait stares at him, unsure. Her thoughts are still slow, still forming. She doesn’t understand the why or the how of her existence, but this tortle does not need her to. He takes out a small vial, golden liquid swirling inside, and with careful precision, begins to paint the cracks in her porcelain skin with the liquid gold. The lines of damage fade as he works, and Cait feels... whole.

The tortle moves with a grace that belies his aged form, his movements almost ritualistic. He hums softly as he works, the sound reverberating in the stillness, filling the space between them with warmth. There is no rush, no urgency. Just the slow, deliberate care of one soul repairing another.

As the tortle finishes, Cait looks down at herself. She is whole again, her body restored with veins of gold tracing through her porcelain form. She is new, but she feels... familiar. The tortle meets her gaze and nods once, as if to say, *you’re not alone.*

Cait opens her mouth to speak, but the words don’t come. There is too much to say and too little understanding yet. Instead, she stands, her body moving smoothly now, fluid where it had once been broken.

The tortle gestures for her to follow him, and Cait does, her steps light on the earth beneath her feet. He leads her to a small alcove, where the remnants of his work lie scattered—tools of repair, shards of broken things, and in the center of it all, a figure like her.

A male.

Kaith, as she will come to know him, lies still, his body whole but unmoving. The tortle’s golden lines run through him too, but there is no spark, no life behind his glassy eyes. Cait steps closer, her fingers hovering over Kaith’s chest, feeling the absence there. She does not understand this grief, but it settles in her all the same.

The tortle touches her arm, his silent presence steady and comforting. They stand together, looking down at Kaith’s unmoving form, two mended souls watching over a third that will never wake.

And in that moment, Cait understands something fundamental. She is alive. She is whole. But she will never be the same.
